Technology use among older adults

Technology surrounds us—from the smartphones we carry almost 24/7 to the walkers and wheelchairs used to help those with mobility impairments to the global positioning system (GPS) devices that help us navigate to our destinations. Too often, the media portrays individuals in US society as being addicted to technology, with myriad negative impacts. The reality, however, is that we are in the infancy stages of understanding the impacts of technology use in general, and for older adults in particular. With the pervasiveness of technology in society, researchers, clinicians, and organizations working with older adults need an in-depth understanding of how older adults are using technology and how it impacts their lives. Researchers are increasingly turning their focus to older adults and technology use, which is beneficial for disciplines studying health and well-being, technology use, and the intersections of these areas—such as sociology, gerontology, psychology, communication, technology/media, and medicine. However, this also presents challenges in staying abreast of the latest research on this topic and ensuring that researchers emerge from their disciplinary silos to cross disciplinary boundaries to learn from each other’s work. Theoretical and empirical advancements in communication and media studies on technological affordances, for example, may inform sociologists and gerontologists seeking to understand the impacts of using technology for older adults or for researchers and outreach organizations who wish to design programs to help older adults use information and communication technologies (ICTs) to enhance their quality of life and engagement with social ties.

This chapter provides an overview of the research on technology use and older adults. In covering a broad range of research related to older adults and technology use, I begin with a brief delineation of two types of technology: ICTs and emerging technologies. I then focus in depth on these types of technologies. To help understand the digital divide and patterns of ICT use among older adults, I situate this work within Elder’s life course paradigm (Elder, 1995). Following this, I call attention to socioeconomic status differences, the challenges of nascent research on ICTs and older adults, impacts of technology use (psychosocial, physical, and unanticipated outcomes), and the gaps in knowledge surrounding research on older adults and technologies. I then explore emerging technologies and the future of aging. In conclusion, I describe challenges facing researchers who study technology and aging and offer a brief call for future research.

Defining technology: information and communication technologies and emerging technologies

At a basic level, technology is defined as a product, process, tool, or application that helps individuals accomplish something. Almost anything could fall under the purview of “technology” given this broad conceptualization. Though the plethora of technologies in today’s society have a range of potential impacts for older adults, I focus primarily on ICT—devices and applications that are used to gather and exchange information as well as communicate with others. ICTs include things such as mobile and smartphones, computers, the Internet, tablets, social network sites, and apps. The range and proliferation of ICTs have increased dramatically in the past decade. Mounting research focuses on understanding the use and impacts of ICTs as well as the causes and consequences of digital inequalities (Robinson et al., 2015). Emerging technologies, of which less is known about use and impacts, are those that have recently been developed and are in the early phases of being disseminated to the public. Examples of emerging technologies include robots, virtual reality, digital voice assistants, augmented reality, smart home technologies, and telehealth applications. Kadylak and Cotten (2020) provide recent statistics on older adults’ willingness to use emerging technologies.

Variations in information and communication technologies use

The types and numbers of technologies continue to evolve, yet not everyone has access or skills to use the range of technologies being developed. Elder’s life course paradigm (1995) provides a frame for understanding how ICT use may vary across the life course. This paradigm allows us to examine how different formative experiences combine with the life course to shape people’s lives. Historical context, the timing of technological developments, and the linking of people’s lives shape the trajectories of ICT use. Within the life course paradigm, distinctions are made between age (e.g., the time since individuals’ birth) and cohorts (Giele & Elder, 1998). Cohorts within this paradigm refer to individuals within a population who experience similar life events within certain periods of time; they are often delineated by birth cohort (Marshall, 1983). Though the specific cohort years and names vary slightly by researchers, older adults typically fall within three cohorts (Zickuhr, 2010): Baby Boomers (born between 1946 and 1964), Silent Generation (born between 1937 and 1945), and the GI Generation (born 1936 or earlier). Note that although the group names include the term “generation,” these groups reflect age cohorts. Age and cohort are often used interchangeably and, unfortunately, they cannot be teased out using cross-sectional data.

Elder’s life course paradigm illustrates the importance of exposure to ICTs as a function of historical timing and proliferation of technologies. Differences in ICT exposure and use exist and also affect the ways in which ICTs are used and which cohorts use them. The timing of technological advances (see Fig. 23.1 for a brief history), stage in the life course, and social tie use and support influence what types of ICTs are used and how they are used in each cohort (Cotten, McCullough, & Adams, 2011; Francis, Kadylak, Cotten, & Rikard, 2016). Boomers, often referred to as digital immigrants, adapted to technological changes as adults, given that many ICTs were developed and proliferated after they entered adulthood; they adjusted to using ICTs in the workplace and altered their communication methods to maintain social ties (Cotten et al., 2011). Particularly for older Boomers, it may have been harder for them to integrate ICTs into their lives as they may not have encountered ICTs until later years in the workforce. For individuals in the Silent and GI Generation cohorts, they are even further distanced from the need to use and integrate ICTs into their lives. Many individuals in these cohorts did not encounter ICTs in the workforce. They historically relied extensively upon landline telephones, as one example, rather than mobile phones (Ling, 2008).

The foundation of the Internet, a prerequisite for development and proliferation of ICTs, occurred in the 1960s; however, the World Wide Web was not opened to the public until 1991 (Internet Hall of Fame, 2019). At this point in history, the youngest GI cohort member was 55, the youngest Silent Generation member was 46, and the youngest Boomer was 27. By the year 2000 only 12% of individuals aged 65 and older, who fall within the GI Generation, and less than 50% of those aged 50–64, who fall within the Silent Generation, reported using the Internet (see Figs. 23.2 and 23.3). By 2016 44% of the GI cohort and between 60% and 75% of the Silent Generation reported using the Internet (see Fig. 23.4, which reports age groups). Though individuals aged 65 and older are increasingly using the Internet, which is often a necessity in order to use many other ICTs and continually evolving emerging technologies, they still lag behind other younger age groups (see Fig. 23.2). Going online allows them to find information to make important life decisions, find health information, and connect with others via online communities and social media (Cotten, 2001; Cotten & Gupta, 2004; Hale, Choi, & Cotten, 2018; Hale, Goldner, Stern, Drentea, & Cotten, 2014). Though being online is important, it is not sufficient for accruing benefits of technology use (Hargittai, 2002; van Deursen & Helsper, 2015); skills are also important (Hunsaker & Hargittai, 2018).

Americans vary widely in their use of the Internet and other ICTs, with particularly marked differentials by age and cohort (see Fig. 23.4). Eight in 10 Boomers use the Internet and ICTs for accessing and sending email, searching the Internet, and getting health information and news (Smith, Anderson, & Page, 2017; Zickuhr & Madden, 2012). ICT devices are widespread among Boomers, with 85% owning a cellphone, 65% having a desktop computer, and 57% owning a laptop (Zickuhr & Madden, 2012). As Boomers retire and no longer have workplace technical support, it is unknown whether they will feel confident enough to continue to use ICTs to enhance their social relationships with others (Berkowsky, 2012). Older Boomers (born between 1946 and 1954), who fall within the population of 65 and older, are less likely to use ICTs or to be as able to use a range of functions with ICTs as their younger Boomer (born between 1955 and 1964) counterparts.

The Silent Generation cohort, the group least likely to be online and which has a slower uptake of ICTs, experienced the technology revolution but spent much of their adult life communicating in ways that did not use the Internet and mobile devices. Among adults aged 75–79, 60% use the Internet, 41% have home broadband services, and 31% own a smartphone. Of those 80 and older, 44% use the Internet, 28% have home broadband services, and only 17% own a smartphone (Anderson & Perrin, 2017). This demographic group tends to use the Internet for a few specific tasks, such as to check email, search the Internet, and obtain health information. Members of this generation may face anxiety when using ICTs, need social or technical support, and are more resistant to trying new ICTs (Czaja et al., 2006; Smith et al., 2017; Tsai et al., 2015).

In both these groups, the most consistent ICTs used include mobile phones, computers, and the Internet. Between 2013 and 2016 smartphone ownership among older adults increased from 18% to 42%. Approximately one-third (32%) of older adults report owning tablet computers (Smith et al., 2017). Fewer older adults use social media, though the percentages have increased in recent years (Anderson & Perrin, 2017). Primary reasons for nonuse include privacy and security concerns, as well as information overload due to the design of many social media sites (Cotten, Yost, Berkowsky, Winstead, & Anderson, 2017). As individuals move into older age in today’s society, they are entering that period in a time where ICT use in general and social media use more specifically have become increasingly prolific, resulting in larger percentages of individuals feeling comfortable with their use. I anticipate that rates of social media use will continue to increase over time among older adults, though this may change as social media and other ICTs’ popularity and interface design continue to evolve over time.

Skills differences have been found among older adults, with younger older adults having more digital skills than older adults (Hargittai, Piper, & Morris, 2018). This is partially due to the fact that those cohorts aged 65–74 are more likely to have encountered ICTs in the workforce and may continue to use them after retirement. Health declines and limitations in instrumental activities of daily living may also lead some older adults to discontinue use over time (Berkowsky, Rikard, & Cotten, 2015; Cotten et al., 2017; Rikard, Berkowsky, & Cotten, 2018).

Research on the types of ICTs older adults use is a first step to understanding technology use in later life; yet another innovative approach is to develop subtypes of technology use, encompassing a range of practices. Quan-Haase, Williams, Kicevski, Elueze, and Wellman (2018), in an in-depth interview study of 41 older adults in Toronto, classified older adults along a continuum of ICT use subtypes, ranging from Non-Users to Reluctants, Apprehensives, Basic Users, Go-Getters, and Savvy Users (for an alternative but also informative typology—which ranges from Technological Resistance Fighters to Technological Evangelists, see Birkland, 2019). Non-Users (10% of sample) did not participate in online activities. Reluctant users (17% of sample) had low digital skills, only engaged in a few online activities at most, and used the Internet for more task-oriented activities. Apprehensive users (17% of sample) also had low digital skills but engaged in slightly more online activities than Reluctants; they expressed interest in learning more digital skills. Basic users, the largest portion of their participants (27% of sample), had moderate digital skills, weren’t motivated to learn new skills, and reported few online activities. Go-Getters (22% of sample) had moderate skills, engaged in a variety of online activities, and seemed optimistic about expanding their digital activities. Savvy users, the smallest segment of their sample (7% of sample), reported high digital skills and engaged in many digital activities. They were also motivated to learn more skills and explore the Internet.

The digital divide

Though it may be narrowing, a digital divide persists, with individuals living in rural areas, having lower income, and being older in age (e.g., 65 and older) being less likely to have access or use technology (see Fig. 23.5; Anderson & Kumar, 2019). Cost is an important aspect of the digital divide for older adults, as is cognitive ability—particularly for those aged 80 and older. Cognitive decline negatively affects older adults, in particular, and their ability to learn to use and/or continue to use ICTs over time (Cotten et al., 2017).

Socioeconomic status has also been associated with whether individuals have access to and use ICTs, as well as how individuals use them (Anderson & Perrin, 2017; Cotten, Francis, Kadylak, et al., 2016; Hargittai & Dobransky, 2017; Smith et al., 2017), with those with lower levels of education and income typically having less access to ICTs as well as lower levels of proficiency with using ICTs. Findings by Pew (Anderson & Perrin, 2017; Smith, 2014; Smith et al., 2017) illustrate that older adults who are younger (ages 65–69), more educated, and have more economic resources are the ones who are more likely to be online, relative to those who are older, less educated, and with fewer economic resources.

The research on cumulative disadvantage indicates that advantages accrue over the life course and early life disadvantages accumulate over time (Elder, 1995; Ferraro & Kelley-Moore, 2003). Differences that start out small early in the life course escalate and widen with age. Early digital advances, such as having access to computers and the Internet, beget subsequent advances—ability to find jobs and complete online applications for jobs; expand educational training, acquisition of more advanced digital skills, ability to use ICTs to overcome spatial and social barriers, and access to a broader array of information to help make important life decisions; and augment ways to maintain connections to strong and weak social ties, thus potentially enhancing social capital and well-being more substantially throughout the life course. The gaps grow over time as the benefits accrue to those with Internet access and skills. Even among individuals who have access to the Internet at a particular point in time, prior experience matters in regard to using ICTs as well as skills in activating resources through the use of ICTs. Social statuses also matter for the digital divide and digital inequalities, as they do for health and other behavioral outcomes. Those on the wrong side of the digital divide, due to socioeconomic status, age, and rural location, do not gain the same benefits from being connected—whether it is in terms of accessing online information, finding jobs, or maintaining social connections. The disadvantages accumulate over time, though few researchers have framed the digital divide in terms of cumulative disadvantage (see Neves, Fonseca, Amaro, & Pasqualotti (2018) for an exception).

Gaps in knowledge and data

Although the Pew Research Center statistics shown in Figs. 23.2–23.5 are the most comprehensive detailing US older adults’ ICT usage, these data have limitations. In most Pew surveys, there are no indicators of the life course, health, social support, or other psychosocial resources that might help researchers examine the processes through which technology use might be related to well-being. These statistics may also overestimate ICT usage rates among older adults, as coverage error likely exists given the sampling methodology includes only community-based samples of individuals who are able to complete telephone and/or Internet surveys (Cotten, 2017; Quinn, 2010). As such, persons in poor physical, cognitive, and/or mental health, and those who are socially isolated, may be underrepresented or missing. This sampling design excludes older adults in nursing and skilled care facilities, as well as other types of care facilities. Pew is not alone in excluding individuals in these types of living environments and those who can’t complete telephone or Internet surveys; most surveys targeting community-dwelling older adults exclude these older adults (Dormagen & Michel, 2018; Fried & Wallace, 1992; Golomb et al., 2012; Groves & Couper, 1998; Herzog & Rodgers, 1992; Korn & Graubard, 1999). However, Pew is the only national polling organization that specifically focuses on technology use in many of their surveys. The majority of other large-scale federally funded survey efforts [see, e.g., the Health and Retirement Surveys (HRS), National Health and Aging Trends Study, General Social Survey, and Wisconsin Longitudinal Study] are omnibus in nature, focusing on a range of health, disability, stress, personality, and other factors, while only including very limited questions examining technology use. Though limited in the technology use measures included (see Table 23.1 for selected data resources), the range of other covariates included and the longitudinal nature of some of these datasets enable better elucidation of the precursors and consequences of ICT use.

ICT research among older adults is still in fairly nascent forms, with many studies using coarse measures and not yet capturing the nuances of ICT uses. For example, the overwhelming majority of studies do not assess how often individuals use ICTs, the purpose and timing of use, nor the level of ICT use, each of which could relate to how ICTs may impact individuals’ lives (Cotten, Goldner, Hale, & Drentea, 2011). Understanding who uses which ICTs and how these trends may change over time is important. However, we must move beyond these basic descriptive studies to more fully examine the specifics, purposes, functions, and modes of use if we are to be able to better assess the impacts and affordances of use on health and aging processes.

In addition, most studies use self-report modes of data collection, which some studies have shown are subject to recall bias, with under- and overestimates of use (Deng et al., 2019; Ellis, Davidson, Shaw, & Geyer, 2018). Given the variety of ICTs that individuals use on a daily basis (e.g., smartphones, laptops, tablets, desktops, and smart watches) as well as the switching between devices, accurately remembering how much time is spent, for example, using email or social media is subject to significant recall bias. While trace data collected from devices and apps would potentially be more accurate, the logistics of this type of data collection are challenging—usually requiring physically changing the devices by installation of an app to log the use (Hampton, 2017), which may not be possible due to privacy concerns, skills to install, or organizational constraints. Even in a recent small-scale study (N = 39) of full-time employed mid-life adults working at a large organization, researchers experienced challenges with app installation and trace data collection due to variability in age and software versions of participant-owned devices, participant digital skills, and inability to install monitoring apps on work devices (Mack, Cotten, Chang, & Bauchspies, 2019). Using a combination of ecological momentary assessments in conjunction with logging apps, time use diaries, and survey self-reports may provide more accurate overall representations of the range of ICT use occurring on a daily basis.

Impacts of technology use for older adults

Though ICT use is increasing among older adults, less is known about the impacts of ICTs on older adults’ lives and how these impacts may change over time. Older adults are at increased risk of loneliness and social isolation, which are both associated with depression (Cacioppo et al., 2002; Cacioppo, Hawkley, & Thisted, 2010; Doman & Le Roux, 2010), suicide (Chang, Sanna, Hirsch, & Jeglic, 2010; Holwerda et al., 2012), and overall mortality (Holwerda et al., 2012; Steptoe, Shankar, Demakakos, & Wardle, 2013). Using ICTs, particularly to maintain contact with social ties, help older adults enhance social connections and well-being (a social causation perspective). Given the high rates of loneliness among older adults (i.e., more than a quarter of older adults aged 70 or older and a third of those aged 60–69 report being lonely; 42.6 million older adults suffer from chronic loneliness; Administration on Aging, 2018), finding ways to decrease loneliness is critical for enhancing well-being among older adults. In addition, approximately 28% (13.8 million) of noninstitutionalized older adults in 2017 lived alone (Administration on Aging, 2018), which is likely to increase social isolation and loneliness. Developing ways to decrease these risks and enhance social connectedness and psychosocial well-being among older adults is critical, particularly with the burgeoning aging population in the United States and around the world. ICTs may be one way to enhance connections and well-being; however, older adults must have access and be able to effectively use ICTs in order to reap the rewards from their use.

While younger generations may feel confident using ICTs to communicate, many older adults are more comfortable and prefer using traditional forms of communication, such as face-to-face interactions and landline phones (Chan, 2015; Ling, 2008; Yuan, Hussain, Hales, & Cotten, 2016). From a preference standpoint, face-to-face encounters are most often preferred by older adults; however, some older adults prefer written communication such as texting. Video and phone calls may be less preferred because of technical difficulties and hearing impairments. Preference may not only be about the availability of communication cues (e.g., aids or prompts that individuals use to signify that they expect some type of response or reaction; they can be verbal or nonverbal cues), but also about individuals’ ability to experience these cues (Yuan et al., 2016). Unfortunately, no studies on older adults have examined whether the psychological benefits are equivalent across modes of interaction. Each ICT has distinct features that are perceived as either beneficial or detrimental for enhancing social connectedness and well-being (Cotten, Francis, et al., 2016; Cotten, Rikard, et al., 2016). Understanding health and contextual constraints on ICT use is important for understanding potential impacts among older adults, particularly over time.

Smartphones, computers, tablets, and other ICT use is at all-time high levels in the United States and around the world; yet, the long-term impacts of ICT use are not well understood. With media outlets and others constantly deriding the prolific use of ICTs, more extensive longitudinal research is needed to understand positive and negative impacts of ICT use for varying age groups in society. In addition, little is known about whether social selection and/or social causation are related to ICT use and varying health impacts, such as psychological distress. Social causation perspectives would argue that ICT use causes poor outcomes, perhaps by exposing older adults to distressing images, exchanges, or information, or displacing social contact in general or taking them away from face-to-face interactions more specifically. Social causation perspectives would suggest that ICT use benefits well-being by enabling older adults to maintain access to social networks, find information to make important life decisions, and stay engaged in society. Social selection perspectives, by contrast, would suggest that a statistical correlation between ICT use and loneliness or depression may reflect the fact that persons with underlying depression or strained social ties are more likely to seek refuge in ICT use or to have weak social ties. According to a social selection perspective, individual factors are the fundamental causes of outcomes (e.g., age, personality), whereas a social causation perspective emphasizes the importance of social conditions as fundamental causes of outcomes (Link & Phelan, 1995). People with fewer friends or who are lonely may turn to the Internet and other ICTs more often (social selection effects), or alternatively Internet use may facilitate interaction with friends and family or enhanced well-being through social ties (social causation effects). Much of the research on fundamental causes has been in relation to health and disease outcomes in particular, especially among medical sociologists (Link & Phelan, 1995). Most researchers in the ICT area do not overtly note whether they approach their studies from a social selection or social causation perspective [for an exception, see Hampton (2019), who uses a nationally representative sample of US adults from the Panel Survey of Income Dynamics].

Psychosocial outcomes

The use of ICTs is one way to promote and maintain social connectedness, and reduce social isolation and loneliness for older adults. ICT proliferation and use have dramatically changed how people communicate with strong and weak social ties. While young adults use various ICTs for communication and fulfillment of social needs (e.g., texting, social media), older adults ICT use consists of email and mobile phone use more so than texting and social media use (Smith, 2014, 2015; Zickuhr, 2010, 2011) for communication with social ties.

Though ICT use is positively associated with older adult well-being (Cotten, Anderson, & McCullough, 2013; Cotten, Ford, Ford, & Hale, 2012; Cotten, Ford, Ford, & Hale, 2014; Forsman & Nordmyr, 2017; Heo, Chun, Lee, Lee, & Kim, 2015; Quintana, Cervantes, Saez, & Isasi, 2018; Yost, Winstead, Berkowsky, & Cotten, 2016), we do not have a rich and complex understanding of the mechanisms through which ICT use influences well-being, as well as potential negative impacts of ICT use for older adults. Research, however, is increasing, which will provide better identification of specific mechanisms through which ICT use impacts well-being. Cotten et al. (2014), using four waves of HRS data, suggest that using ICTs to communicate with social ties may be one of the main ways in which ICT use impacts well-being. Relatedly, Chopik (2016) finds that the benefits of ICT use for physical and psychological health are mediated by reduced loneliness. Given increased mobility and health limitations often experienced by older adults, using ICTs may allow them to enhance well-being by overcoming spatial and social barriers (Cotten et al., 2017; Winstead et al., 2013), which allows them to maintain contact with their social ties, find information online to help them make life decisions, and exchange support with their social ties. While Cotten et al. (2014) focus specifically on depression, others have focused on isolation, loneliness, social engagement, social support, mattering, life satisfaction, psychological distress, and other well-being outcomes (Berkowsky, 2012; Chopik, 2016; Cotten et al., 2013; Francis et al., 2016; Francis, Rikard, Cotten, & Kadylak, 2019; Hampton, 2019; Heo et al., 2015; Ihm & Hsieh, 2015; Meshi, Cotten, & Bender, 2019; Szabo, Allen, Stephens, & Alpass, 2019).

Instrumental ICT use (e.g., using ICTs to access services, information, or opportunities that may not be available through other ways) has been related to higher social engagement, measured as participation in social gatherings that were offline (visiting family, seeing friends, volunteering, and so on), among older adults in the greater Chicago area (Ihm & Hsieh, 2015). For residents of assisted and independent living communities, ICT use promoted a sense of mattering (e.g., the perception by an individual of being important, acknowledged, and relied upon by social ties; Rosenberg & McCullough, 1981) through social connectedness (Francis et al., 2016; Francis et al., 2019); mattering mediated the relationship between ICT use and depression. ICT use may connect continuing care retirement community residents with support networks, which enhances their sense of mattering. Mattering, in turn, may serve as a safeguard against symptoms of depression as it represents an individual’s recognition of his or her value and importance to others (Francis et al., 2016; Francis et al., 2019). Other researchers have found that ICT use (e.g., social network use) was associated with better health and well-being (Chopik, 2016). This link was mediated by reduced levels of loneliness originating from ICT use.

Longitudinal analyses of the effects of ICT use on well-being are smaller in number than cross-sectional studies, particularly for older adult samples. Cotten et al. (2012, 2014), using multiple waves of HRS data, found that Internet/email use was related to lower depression rates over time, even when controlling for prior depression levels. In their 2014 longitudinal analyses using four waves of HRS data, Cotten and colleagues’ (2014) statistical method allowed past depression to affect current depression. They also used a matching technique to ensure adequate covariate overlap, finding that Internet use effects remained intact and unchanged across the waves. These results also suggest that contact with social ties is one mechanism through which Internet use affects depression. In a longitudinal analysis of three waves of the New Zealand Health, Work, and Retirement Study, Szabo et al. (2019) found that older adults’ social ICT use (i.e., connecting with friends and family) was related to well-being, through decreasing loneliness and increasing social engagement. Informational (e.g., reading online health-related materials) and instrumental (e.g., online banking) online activities were related to enhanced well-being, though the pathway was through engagement in wider activities rather than decreased loneliness. Szabo and colleagues’ measure of well-being focused on control, autonomy, self-realization, and pleasure, which is different from many of the studies focusing on the psychosocial impacts of ICT use on older adults or depression. Additional studies that include a range of differentiated measures, focusing on specific aspects of mental health, social support, life satisfaction, social ties, and self-efficacy, rather than more global measures of well-being and social connection, are needed to further differentiate the ways through which specific types of ICT use impact well-being and the specific mechanisms through which these impacts occur.

Physical health outcomes

Though studies increasingly are focused on psychosocial well-being outcomes of ICT use, very little is known about physical health outcomes. Theoretically, using online health resources could help older adults to better manage health conditions and engage in healthy behaviors by finding information about ways to increase healthy activities, researching health conditions and medications, or through telemedicine activities (Cotten & Gupta, 2004; Samal et al., 2011). In a cross-sectional study, Sims, Reed, and Carr (2017) examined two self-reported aspects of physical health—subjective health and functional limitations—among adults aged 80 and older. They reported that using more devices and applications was associated with better physical health, both subjective health and fewer functional limitations. Using ICTs for social reasons, however, was not related to either of these physical health outcomes, though using ICTs to learn new information and skills was associated with better physical health outcomes. Overall ICT use was related to physical health outcomes through the mediating role of using ICTs to learn new information and skills. It is likely that using ICTs, particularly for online health information seeking, telehealth activities, or connection with online support groups could enhance physical health by helping motivate older adults to engage in healthy behaviors. However, as ICT use continues to increase among older adults, there may be negative impacts on physical health, such as repetitive motion issues. Cotten and Gupta (2004) found that individuals who reported being healthier and happier were less likely to search for health information exclusively online. The majority of individuals, regardless of whether they sought health information online or offline, reported relying on healthcare professionals as a primary source for health information. Given the increased uptake in ICT use and online health information seeking that has occurred since this early study, more recently, researchers have found a greater percentage of individuals report turning to the Internet as the first place they seek health information, when compared to healthcare providers, social ties, and traditional media (Jacobs, Amuta, & Jeon, 2017). Additional research is clearly warranted focusing on physical health outcomes of ICT use, as well as how social selection and social causation are related to both ICT use and physical health outcomes.

Unanticipated outcomes

As noted above, much of the research on effects of ICT use focuses on well-being and social connection outcomes. Though research is increasing on unanticipated and/or negative impacts of technology use for other demographic groups (Chotpitayasunondh & Douglas, 2016; He, Turel, & Bechara, 2018; LaRose, Connolly, Lee, Li, & Hales, 2014; Nijssen, Schaap, & Verheijen, 2018; Stadin et al., 2016; Turel, Cavagnaro, & Meshi, 2018), these issues within samples of older adults have received less attention.

One area that is underexplored is how ICT use relates to relationship communication, satisfaction, and conflict. The expectations for communication modality, frequency, and content may be a source of intergenerational conflict (Clarke, Preston, Raksin, & Bengtson, 1999). Perceptions of copresence (e.g., the sense of connection to another person’s awareness, whether in the same physical space or not) may matter for older adults and be affected by others’ use of ICTs. Older adults’ use of ICTs is affected by their distaste for the proliferation of ICT use by others in social settings. Ancillary ICT use during face-to-face interactions can diminish the intimacy of that interaction and create a disruption of copresence which leaves the older adult feeling ignored and disconnected (Kadylak et al., 2018). Phubbing, using smartphones in social settings and interacting with the phone rather than other people present, adversely impacts older adults (Błachnio & Przepiorka, 2018; Kadylak et al., 2018). Phubbing may increase social isolation and feelings of being ignored among older adults. Building on digital divide research, we have termed this gap between ICT engaged generations and older adults the physical–digital divide (Ball et al., 2017). Given the lack of longitudinal research in this area, it is possible that endogeneity is a concern; in other words, those with poorer quality relationships may be more vulnerable to phubbing and experiencing a physical–digital divide—rather than the reverse. Similarly, children and grandchildren may phub more frequently if they have poor-quality relationships with their parents or grandparents. Future longitudinal studies can help to further elucidate these relationships.

Higher levels of technostress, stress created by ICT use as individuals deal with the changing cognitive and social requirements of using ICTs, are associated with older age (Nimrod, 2018; Tarafdar, Tu, Ragu-Nathan, & Ragu-Nathan, 2007). Changing interfaces, remembering and updating passwords, and maintaining online safety and privacy are challenges for older adults (Cotten et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2016).

Related to the stress that can result from these technology changes, technology failures and maintenance can be challenging for older adults (Cotten et al., 2017). Though not focusing on older adults, Gonzales (2016, 2017) suggests that as ICTs continue to proliferate in our society a new element of the digital divide will exist related to technology maintenance. Maintaining access to ICTs often requires a range of resources—from financial to social resources. Individuals with low incomes may have not have resources to maintain device connections, nor repair ICTs when damaged. Although some programs exist to help low-income individuals acquire Internet access or mobile phones, to the best of my knowledge, no federal programs or policies exist to facilitate ICT maintenance for low-income individuals or older adults.

When ICT technical difficulties and hassles arise, older adults consistently reach out to social ties for assistance to grapple with these challenges (Francis, Kadylak, Makki, Rikard, & Cotten, 2018; Hunsaker et al., 2019). However, older adults are often dependent upon social ties’ availability to provide technical support (Hunsaker et al., 2019). The interaction with both family members and technical support representatives results in tangible support for older adults. In this sense, the otherwise frustrating experience of ICT difficulty inadvertently serves as a “catalyst to connection” and social support for older adults (Francis, Kadylak, et al., 2018).

Cyberbullying and online exploitation of older adults are also topics ripe for examination. Media coverage of the latest phone and online scams targeting individuals, and older adults more specifically, are becoming commonplace. From calls about a social tie being in jail and needing money to be released to technical support offers to phishing attacks targeting computers, the range of scams is increasing, with perpetrators residing in foreign countries as well as in local communities (National Council on Aging, 2019; U.S. Senate Special Committee on Aging, 2019).

Compared to younger cohorts, older adults, in general, are less likely to experience or participate in cyberbullying. Yet, older adults express a high level of anxiety that they will experience financial exploitation and cyberbullying (Alhabash, Cotten, Tsai, Rifon, & LaRose, 2014; Barlett & Chamberlin, 2017; Barlett, Madison, Heath, & DeWitt, 2019; Jiang et al., 2016; Kowalski, Limber, & McCord, 2018). Older adults are concerned about online safety and security, perceive themselves to be more vulnerable to online safety threats, and are less confident in their abilities to maintain online safety; they tend to rely on social ties for assistance when they encounter online safety issues (Alhabash et al., 2014; Jiang et al., 2016). The Federal Trade Commission (2018) reports that older adults are more likely to report instances of fraud than are younger age groups.

Technology overuse may also become relevant as more older adults use ICTs and comfort and skills increase with use. Though media and some researchers use the term “addiction,” I do not. What is normative technology use for one individual or group may not be normative for another; however, this does not mean that someone is addicted to technology use. I define technology overuse as a level of use that interferes with activities of daily living. For instance, if an individual constantly checks social media to the exclusion of completing normal activities, such as work, running errands, visiting with social ties, and so forth, then this is problematic. Meshi et al. (2019) recently found that problematic social media usage was associated with perceived social isolation; while a causal relationship cannot be inferred from this cross-sectional study, longitudinal studies examining ICT use, isolation, and activities of daily living could help to parse out these relationships.

The current public discourse surrounding technology addiction focuses largely on teens and young adults, due in part to ageist assumptions about how older adults engage with new and emerging technologies. Though research noted earlier in this chapter shows that a digital divide still exists among older adults, it is important to acknowledge the heterogeneity of use within this group. Though there are many older adults who use ICTs minimally or in basic ways, there are certainly highly prolific ICT users, such as the Go-Getters identified by Quan-Haase et al. (2018), within the older adult demographic group as well. It will be important to continue to examine the heterogeneous nature of ICT use within older adults in general and specific cohorts, as well as examine whether technology overuse is a problematic outcome for segments of these groups.

Gaps in knowledge

As individuals move into retirement and older age, they are at increased risk of experiencing a range of health issues that may affect their ability to effectively function in society. Current cohorts of older adults (older Boomers and the Silent Generation) are less likely to be ICT users, relative to the cohorts that follow them, though some research suggests that members of these groups are rapidly crossing the digital divide (Smith, 2014). As my colleagues and I have noted, efforts are needed to help these older adults who are being left behind to overcome the digital divide, so that they can reap the potential benefits of using ICTs (Berkowsky, Cotten, Yost, & Winstead, 2013; Cotten et al., 2014; Winstead et al., 2013). However, it matters how older adults engage in the digital world (Szabo et al., 2019). Encouraging older adults to search for information to help them make important decisions, as well as engage with social ties in ways that can lead to the exchange of social support and integration into society, are likely to be the most promising ways to enhance well-being. Further research into the physical health impacts of ICT use is clearly warranted, given the paucity of focus on this topic.

Emerging technologies and the future of aging

While the earlier sections have focused specifically on ICT use, I now turn to emerging technologies and how they may enable successful aging. See Kadylak and Cotten (2020) for recent research focused on older adults’ willingness to use several types of emerging technologies. New technologies are developed every day, yet many do not make it to market. Attending mobile health and/or ehealth conferences [e.g., Connected Health, Healthcare Information and Management Systems Society (HiMSS), MobiHealth] or the Consumer Electronics Show that are, in reality, more like tradeshows than conferences reveals the range and proliferation of the new technologies being developed. Most of the products showcased at these conferences are created by for-profit companies hoping to develop the next best-selling device, app, or other tool to improve people’s lives. Many of the products being developed never make it to the mass distribution market due to lack of business development plans, product differentiation, and marketing strategy, as well as market saturation and challenges acquiring venture capital (Storey & Wallace, 2019). The products that reach the marketplace are rarely inexpensive, and the vast majority are not covered by insurance, thus potentially perpetuating or exacerbating disparities. Among the many emerging technologies to choose from, I discuss four areas in this section that are likely to be particularly relevant for older adults in the coming decade.

Robotics

Robotic applications are increasing, particularly in relation to activities related to older adults. These robotic applications range from Roombas (designed to clean floors) to devices that automatically dispense medications at certain times to robotic pets and assistive robots which help with specific care activities. Given the caregiving shortages that exist in the United States and around the world, robots may yield one adaptive way to cope with the burgeoning older adult population, especially older adults with functional limitations. Japan has been a leader in developing robots to assist with successful aging and caregiving (Headquarters for Japan’s Economic Revitalization, 2015), due perhaps in part to a rapidly increasing aging population and a labor shortage resulting from a declining workforce-age population and historically restrictive immigration policies.

Whether it is through telepresence robots or assistive robots, robotic applications have the potential to help older adults engage in communication with caregivers and social ties, decrease functional limitations, and enhance mobility and activities of daily living assistance (Cotten et al., 2017; Francis, Hussein, & Cotten, 2018; Stuck, Hartley, Mitzner, Beer, & Rogers, 2017). As robotic developments continue to evolve, developers and researchers need to understand user needs across diverse populations, be cognizant of ethical issues of using robots with disadvantaged and vulnerable populations, and advance adaptive and autonomous robot features (Garcia et al., 2019). As with most emerging technologies, challenges remain around maintenance and battery life, as well as acceptance of robots (Lee, Tan, & Sabanovic, 2016; National Science & Technology Council, 2019). Only 37% of individuals aged 65 and older reported they would be interested in using a robot to help with caregiving for themselves or their family members (Smith & Anderson, 2017). As robotic applications become more pervasive in society and older adults are exposed to them more frequently, attitudes toward them will likely improve and willingness to use them will likely increase.

Internet of things

Another important consideration regarding older adults and successful aging has to do with what is known as the Internet of Things (IoT), which refers to how smart devices and applications work together or are connected through Wi-Fi. In essence, these devices and applications communicate with each other. Some examples include IoT systems to control digital assistants, lights, thermostats, blinds and shades, and cameras installed in doors that show who is at the door, in addition to providing means for individuals to unlock the door remotely (Cotten et al., 2017). A range of sensors and cameras are increasingly being integrated into homes as well as wearable devices, enabling medication reminders, remote monitoring, sensing movement via types of physical activities and movement throughout homes, monitoring sleep, detecting falls, and enabling people to monitor their heart rate, blood pressure, and other health indicators. IoT systems are typically controlled via apps installed on smartphones and/or tablet computers.

When these IoT systems, devices, and applications are used by older adults or their caregivers, it may enable them to make more time-relevant decisions about health and healthcare needs, as well as have increased control over their living and social environments—which should theoretically enhance independent living (Cotten et al., 2017). They can also be used to alert caregivers and social ties when activity levels change. However, privacy and security concerns are still an issue as recent media reports indicate some of these connected devices may gather data that users are not aware they are gathering.

For older adults to successfully age in place, housing must be amenable to accommodations that occur due to changes in health and mobility—including installation of assistive devices such as the ones discussed in this section. Housing quality matters, both in terms of ability for modifications and accommodations but also in terms of the high-speed Internet connections which are needed for many IoT devices. Older adults face significant challenges related to housing, including accessibility, affordability, and maintenance, as well as a lack of existing homes suited for individuals with disabilities (HUD Office of Policy Development & Research, 2017). While housing quality and the capacity to age in place are linked with technology, we should not forget that a digital divide still exists, with many older adults being on the wrong side of the digital divide, and that maintenance of these IoT systems may be a challenge for many older adults who are not skilled in use of technologies (Cotten, 2019).

Telehealth

Given mobility and driving impairments among many older adults, telehealth may be one option to enhance health monitoring and outcomes for older adults. Though a variety of definitions exist for telehealth, it primarily consists of “methods for delivering and enhancing healthcare, public health, and health education delivery and support using telecommunications technologies” (National Science & Technology Council, 2019, p. 20). Those receiving care can be at varied locations, rather than traveling to a specific healthcare site. Current tools for telehealth include telephone, email, secure messaging to collect and send health information, wireless monitoring systems, case management tools such as text messaging and apps, and video tools which allow care providers access to patients from remote locations (Totten et al., 2016). Though telehealth applications are diverse, telehealth has been shown to be effective for three specific uses: (1) remotely monitoring patients who have chronic conditions; (2) counseling of and communicating with patients with chronic conditions; and (3) conducting behavioral health psychotherapy (Totten et al., 2016). In many telehealth applications, a healthcare provider does the monitoring of patients; however, they are also useful for caregivers who seek to monitor and/or provide care remotely. As technology continues to evolve, it is likely that other technologies will be used in caring for patients. Examples of such technologies include virtual reality, augmented reality, wearables and sensors, robots, and artificial intelligence (Phillips, Lee, Quinn, & Hylton, 2019). As I noted earlier, assistance is often needed in order for older adults to be able to successfully use technologies. The burden of this assistance is likely to fall upon family caregivers, perhaps exacerbating or adding an additional layer of caregiving burden. For those who are aging alone, a potential lack of technological support may result in less successful telemedicine opportunities for older adults who are not technologically skilled. Further research is needed on this topic.

A challenge with increasing telehealth services arises due to inconsistencies in legislation (Phillips et al., 2019): individual states’ requirements for patients to access telehealth as well as reimbursement policies vary dramatically. Similarly, services that are reimbursable through telehealth and provider types eligible for reimbursement also vary. Legislative and policy consistency across the United States could enhance the use of telehealth services by healthcare providers and patients. For a list of what each state offers in terms of telehealth, see the Center for Connected Health Policy website at https://www.cchpca.org/telehealth-policy/current-state-laws-and-reimbursement-policies.

Driving and vehicle advances

As people age, physical changes occur in driving-related capabilities. Older adults experience slower reaction times, deteriorating eyesight and hearing, and declining muscle strength and flexibility (National Institute on Aging, 2019). Due to eyesight and other declines, some older adults refrain from driving at night or in unfamiliar areas, reduce distance and number of trips, and adapt driving speed (Baldock, Mathias, McLean, & Berndt, 2006; Pristavec, 2018). When physical impairments increase, they may give up driving completely.

Older adults are keeping their driver’s license and driving longer distances than in earlier points in history, though some are cutting back or adapting their driving practices (Pristavec, 2018). Driving frequency of older adults is related to social tie visitation, attendance at religious services, and social participation (Pristavec, 2018). Sixteen percent of individuals aged 65 and older were involved in traffic fatalities in 2017, with percentages increasing as age increases among older adults (Insurance Information Institute, 2019). Impairments in cognition, vision, and motor function are primarily responsible for increased crash rates among individuals aged 65 and older. Vehicle advances may help older adults overcome some of these challenges.

With the increasing number of adaptive safety systems and driver assist functions in vehicles, as well as connected and autonomous vehicles, the way that older adults use vehicles is already changing. Automatic braking, adaptive cruise control, lane departure detection, and other safety features of new vehicles may make it easier for older adults to continue to drive longer and safer. As vehicle detection and safety features continue to evolve, it is likely that vehicles will be a conduit through which declines in cognitive and physical abilities can be detected in the future (National Science & Technology Council, 2019). The National Institutes of Health recently approved this as an area for funding.

Given that giving up driving is one of the major stressful life events for older adults, especially for socially isolated older adults who do not have social ties who can drive them places, autonomous vehicles may be an emerging technology that can enhance older adults’ ability to stay engaged in society—through enabling older adults to continue to participate in community events and shopping, visit with social ties, and engage in entertainment activities (Bailey et al., 2018; Yankelevich et al., 2018). However, for those with mobility impairments that necessitate assistive devices to enhance mobility, autonomous vehicles must be equipped for wheelchairs, walkers, and other mobility devices, and either individuals or robots of some type may be needed to help older adults with mobility impairments to enter and exit from autonomous vehicles. Similarly, for individuals with vision impairments, modifications in applications used to summon autonomous vehicles, as well as design of the vehicles, may be needed. Autonomous vehicle manufacturers and entities that use autonomous vehicles must take these considerations into account when designing the vehicles and when disseminating them to various locales and groups.

Most older adults currently report that they would not be willing to ride in an autonomous vehicle; only 81% of older adults aged 65 and older in a representative sample of older adults in the United States reported they would be willing to ride in an autonomous vehicle in the future (Kadylak & Cotten, 2019). Pew Research Center survey data shows that only 35% of those aged 50 and older report they would want to ride in a driverless car if they were given the opportunity (Smith & Anderson, 2017). Significant outreach is needed to help older adults understand what autonomous vehicles are, their safety features, and how they can benefit older adults, before older adults will be willing to use them in the future. While autonomous vehicles are being used in pilots in various parts of the United States and around the world, it will still be close to a decade at least before they are more widely disseminated in the United States (Yankelevich et al., 2018). Once they become more prolific in society, it is likely that people’s attitudes toward them will change as they see more people using them for transportation.

Future research and conclusions

New technologies are being created around the world every day. Many of these technologies will help older adults live at home, stay connected to their social ties and caregivers, overcome functional limitations, continue to be mobile as they age, and allow them to actively engage in society through driving, buying products, socializing, being entertained, and finding information. In this section, I briefly suggest a few areas that I see as being critical for expanding research on older adults and technologies.

The majority of existing datasets have very basic information on technology use. Measures of email, Internet, phone, and perhaps social media use are periodically included. However, almost none of these datasets include information about the types, functions, timing, and motivations of use; nor do they include change in use over time and the reasons for these changes. As shown in Table 23.1, even in omnibus surveys that include ICT use measures, these measures are cursory at best. Longitudinal studies are needed that focus on a range of technologies, the multimodal nature of technology use (e.g., people check email on mobile phones, desktops, laptops, tablets, and smart watches—often in the span of one day), functions and outcomes of use, as well as a range of behavioral and health outcomes. Having access to device use data in addition to self-report data is critical given the range and proliferation of devices in society as well as recall bias that is inherent given the prevalence of multimodal technology use.

Various theories on aging suggest that as individuals move into older adulthood, they consciously refine their social networks. For instance, Carstensen (1993) uses socioemotional selectivity to argue that with diminishing time horizons, older adults pare down their number of social contacts. This theory, however, was developed at a point in time when people visited others in person or talked through landline telephones. Given the increasing number of older adults using social media, as well as smartphones, Facetime, and Skype, does this theory still hold at this point in history? How do older adults perceive virtual social ties, and can they substitute for “real-life” interaction? Examining older adults’ views on social networks, virtual relations, and interaction preferences is needed for diverse groups of older adults.

The increasing proliferation and use of digital assistants and wearables is an area where further research is also needed. In addition to privacy and security concerns with these devices, determining the best ways to use the massive amounts of data being generated from these devices is critical if we are to understand whether user behavior is changed and the potential for these devices to enhance health and quality of life. However, this will also necessitate that device manufacturers work with researchers to share data, which to date has been hard to secure for the majority of researchers seeking to implement studies using these devices. Related to this, having social scientists work with computer scientists to mine device data and understand the potential of use data to predict future health changes will be critical.

Unfortunately, it is likely that scams and cybersecurity threats will continue to proliferate, given the high levels of technology use in the United States and around the world. Understanding ways to keep older adults safe when using devices is critical, as is working with device manufacturers to ensure that interface design and safety mechanisms are intuitive for older adults and those with cognitive decline.

Some of the existing and emerging technologies may help individuals who are caring for older adults—whether it is through accessing their homes remotely, doing virtual check-ins, or ensuring that mobility patterns are not changing. However, caregiver shortages are increasing, particularly among direct care workers (e.g., nursing assistants, home health aides, and personal care aids). Projections are that by 2030 there will be 3.4 million direct care jobs in the United States, with a severe lack of individuals available to fill these jobs (U.S. Department of Health, & Human Services, Health Resources & Services Administration, Bureau of Health Workforce, National Center for Health Workforce Analysis, 2018). Approximately 50% of the direct care jobs are in home and community settings. Caregiver shortages may push the development of new technologies, resulting in increased demand for safe and secure technologies that allow monitoring and care of older adults across space and time. All of these things may help older adults to age in place more successfully, with support provided through telehealth or virtual engagement of social ties, caregivers, and direct care workers. It will be interesting from a policy perspective to see if ICTs such as these are someday viewed as on par with things like hearing aids and glasses as technologies necessary to one’s health and engagement, and thus covered through public programs such as Medicare.

Research is needed that examines the interrelationships among caregiving, technology use, and monitoring and care of older adults in order to determine whether and/or how technology use may facilitate caregiving and monitoring of older adults over time.

Given the rapidly changing technological world in which we live, we need more people to study not just what types of individuals are using various technologies or how they are using them, but also to focus on the cumulative advantage and disadvantage impacts of technologies in general and as people move through the life course. By gaining an understanding of how different types of technology use affect our lives, hopefully we can begin to design interventions to improve quality of life for varying groups in our society. In conjunction with interventions designed to improve quality of life, tailored training programs are needed to help many older adults cross the digital divide and to enable them to persist in using technologies over time so that they can reap the potential benefits of using ICTs (Berkowsky et al., 2013; Cotten et al., 2014; Cotten et al., 2017; Winstead et al., 2013). Classification systems such as those by Birkland (2019) and Quan-Haase et al. (2018) could potentially inform the development of such tailored interventions designed to help older adults overcome the digital divide and gain the most benefits from technology use, which will hopefully enhance their quality of life and successful aging.

Helping older adults cross the digital divide is important; however, maintenance of devices among older adults is an understudied area. As my colleagues and I have reported elsewhere (Cotten et al., 2017; Francis, Kadylak, et al., 2018; Houston, Richardson, & Cotten, 2019), when ICTs break down, require software updates, require password updates, and software interfaces change, this can be stressful for older adults. If social ties or professionals are not available to assist in these situations, older adults may not continue to be online and use ICTs. Groups and organizations seeking to help older adults cross the digital divide and maintain use over time, as well as researchers studying the use and impacts of ICTs, must also factor in the cognitive and emotional challenges associated with ICT maintenance (Gonzales, 2016; Gonzales, Ems, & Suri, 2016; van Deursen & van Dijk, 2019). As the Boomers grow older, it will be interesting to see how their use evolves as new technologies continue to be developed and disseminated, and whether they will be able to maintain the types and functions of use of various ICTs.

Technology is constantly evolving, and researchers are only in the infancy stage of understanding the impacts of technology use on individuals, groups, and society more generally. Overcoming methodological and sampling challenges of studying older adults (Birkland & Kaarst-Brown, 2010; Cotten, 2017), in addition to evolving technology challenges, are critical if we are to advance research on technologies and older adults. Better cooperation between technology companies and researchers is needed if we are to advance research in understanding the myriad ways in which individuals use ICTs, as well as the complexity of the potential mechanisms through which ICT use may impact people’s lives. I encourage others to think about ways to further our understanding of the impacts of our technologically mediated lives and how we can harness the power of technology to enhance health and well-being for everyone.
