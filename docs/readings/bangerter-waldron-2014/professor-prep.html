Discussion question 01

Summarize the central finding in one sentence.

30-second answer

Qualitative interviews with long-distance grandparents identified 100 unique turning points in eight categories, while the Retrospective Interview Technique yielded five closeness trajectories. Events varied in their perceived positive or negative implications. The findings show diverse relationship paths and communication challenges during grandchildren's adolescence, as recalled by grandparents; they do not establish a single developmental course or causal effects.

This study examines changes in long-distance relationships between grandparents and their adolescent grandchildren by identifying relational turning points and trajectories. Qualitative analysis of data collected from interviews with grandparents yielded 100 unique turning points. Constant comparative analysis revealed eight distinct categories of relational turning points; Spending Time Together, Family Relational Dynamics, Geographic Distance, Lack of Relational Investment, Use of Technology, Relational Investment, Lack of Free Time, and Grandchild Gaining Independence. These varied in the degree to which they positively or negatively impacted relational closeness. Application of the Retrospective Interview Technique (RIT) yielded five distinctive relational trajectories: Decrease in Closeness, Increase in Closeness, Multidimensional Changes in Closeness, Minimal Changes in Closeness, and Consistent Relational Closeness. The results expose the communicative challenges faced by long-distance-grandparents, the diversity of these relationships, and the ways in which grandparenting bonds change over time. Implications for an enriched understanding of grandparenting relationships and practical applications for families are explored.

Discussion question 02

What did the two research questions ask?

30-second answer

RQ1 asked how grandparent-grandchild closeness changes during the grandchild's adolescence. RQ2 asked which relational events grandparents most identify as positive or negative turning points with a long-distance adolescent grandchild. The authors anticipated variation but did not specify the exact trajectories in advance. The questions therefore guided an exploratory analysis of grandparents' perspectives.

Evidence: RATIONALE-001

Read the source passage

Grandparent–grandchild relationships are likely to change, particularly as a grandchild moves through adolescence. However, we know little about the trajectories of these relationships as they are experienced by grandparents. Do they consistently decrease in closeness, as some researchers have speculated (e.g. Attar-Schwartz et al., 2009) due to the grandchildren becoming invested in their peer groups and school commitments? It may be that some relationships actually become closer over time and others cycle between states of closeness and distance in response to life events (Holladay et al., 1998). Thus, we anticipated that grandparents would report varying trajectories of relational closeness and that these trajectories would be contingent on life events of both the grandchild and grandparent as well as events within the family. At the same time, the exact nature of these trajectories could not be predicted, so we posed Research Question 1 to guide our analysis.

RQ1. In what ways does the closeness of grandparent–grandchild relationships change over the course of the grandchild's adolescence?

Holladay et al. (1998) found that geographic separation from a grandmother identified as a negative turning point for granddaughters. These findings encouraged us to examine turning points in long distance relationships exclusively. Grandparents shoulder much responsibility for maintaining the emotional vitality of these bonds; they presumably develop unique relational insights about the family dynamics that shape the relationship. Yet, researchers have yet to study turning points from a grandparent's perspective. Our second research question focused on identifying the specific turning points that shape these relational trajectories. Based on past research, we certainly anticipated geographic distance to play a unique role in the relational events reported by grandparents and suspected that grandparents who positively adapt to the geographic distance may maintain higher levels of relational quality with their grandchildren over time. However, given that other possibilities were likely to surface, our efforts were guided by this research question.

RQ2. What types of relational events do grandparents most identify as positive and negative turning points in their relationships with their long distance adolescent grandchild?

Evidence: METHOD-001

Read the source passage

Participants were recruited through collaboration with the Osher Lifelong Learning Institute, a noncredit university-sponsored educational program designed for older members of the local community. All current participants of the Osher Lifelong Learning Institute at Arizona State University were sent an email or provided handouts describing the study and eligibility for participation. Eligibility for participation required participants to have at least one grandchild between the ages of 12 and 19 and to agree with the following statement, adapted from Guldner and Swensen (1995): “At least one of my grandchildren lives far enough away from me that it would be very difficult or impossible to see them every day”.

Qualitative, semi-structured interviews were conducted with thirty-five (N = 35) participants in their homes and on the campus of a large university. Five interviews could not be transcribed. The ages of participants ranged from 60 to 82 years (M = 70.9, SD = 5.34) while the ages of the grandchildren ranged between 12 and 19 (M = 14.7, SD = 2.36). Twenty-nine participants identified their race/ethnicity as Caucasian, one participant was identified as Hispanic. All participants lived independent of professional caregivers; twenty-three participants lived either part time or full time in a residential retirement community while seven participants lived in homes outside of retirement communities. Twenty-one participants were female (N = 21), and nine participants were male (N = 9). Twenty-six participants were married and four participants were identified as single/widowed/divorced. Our previous studies of this population indicated that questions about income tended to make participants uncomfortable, so we refrained from asking. However, the larger population OSHER life-long learning students were consistently identified as having a “mid-range” income, and homes in the retirement community sell for prices near or slightly above the national average. Most residents have completed at least some college and roughly 50% have college degrees. Thus, our sample consists primarily of middle class, married, fairly-well educated retired persons.

Discussion question 05

What did the Retrospective Interview Technique capture?

30-second answer

Grandparents identified turning points and plotted changes in closeness for one selected long-distance grandchild, from age five to the child's current age. Closeness was defined as the psychological bond the grandparent felt toward the grandchild. Interviews lasted roughly seven to 45 minutes, and 30 hand-drawn graphs were digitized. These were retrospective reconstructions, rather than repeated observations collected throughout childhood.

Evidence: PROCEDURE-001

Read the source passage

Turning points were generated using the Retrospective Interview Technique (RIT), in which informants are interviewed about turning points that have taken place within the relationship. Participants were then asked to graph those turning points on a grid in order to generate an understanding of how the relationship has changed over time and what events caused these changes (Baxter & Pittman, 2001). RIT is a particularly interactive methodology that allows the participant and the researcher to gain a visual representation of the relationship and is especially suitable for building participant/researcher rapport. Thus, this strategy is appropriate for gaining a valid understanding of relational events that shape the closeness of grandparents and their grandchildren and allows these events to be understood from a developmental perspective.

The term ‘closeness’ was isolated and discussed at great length in order to ensure that participants understood this important aspect of the study. Consistent with Golish (2000), closeness was defined as the psychological bond that a grandparent feels towards the grandchild. Once it was evident that participants understood the definition of relational closeness, the interview shifted to discuss and define turning points that cause changes in relational closeness. The theoretical concept of relational turning points was thoroughly discussed and participants were given several examples of turning points cited from previous research studies (e.g. Holladay et al., 1998) such as a death in the family, marriage, moving out of state, or the birth of a child. Defining these terms early in the interview process ensured that participants were able to think about these relationships in ways that correlate to the purpose of the research study.

Next, participants were asked to select one of their long-distance grandchildren to discuss during the interview. They were then presented with a RIT graph that included the level of closeness on the y-axis (1 — most close; 5 — least close) and the age of the child on the x-axis. Participants plotted changes in relational closeness beginning at age five until the grandchild's current age. Beginning the study of relational closeness at age five was justified not only by similar retrospective research, (Golish, 2000) but also by Piaget's theory of the preoperational stage of cognitive development. At the preoperational stage, which occurs around age five, a child produces mental abilities that are essential to relational development (Miller & Church, 2003). The interview then transitioned to a discussion of each turning point. Interviews ranged from approximately 7 min to 45 min in length. Immediately following the interview, a transcript was made from the recording, yielding 116 pages of double-spaced text. The thirty hand-drawn RIT graphs were converted to digital displays (see Figs. 1–5).

Discussion question 07

How was constant comparative analysis conducted?

30-second answer

Each turning point was compared with earlier ones, with coding notes explaining grouping and separation. The first author developed initial categories, refined definitions through discussion with the second author and several grandparents, and reapplied the scheme while searching for negative cases. Saturation appeared after roughly 75% of the data, but all data were coded. The graphs were similarly grouped by patterns of reported change.

Evidence: ANALYSIS-001

Read the source passage

The constant comparative method (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) utilizes comparing and contrasting as the main intellectual tools for establishing boundaries of categories and discerning conceptual similarities between categories (Tesch, 1990). We utilized constant comparative method to identify a typology of turning points and relational trajectories identified by participants. Each turning point was compared with the previous turning points in an effort to identify its similarities and differences. Coding notes documented the reasons for grouping and separating turning point descriptions. The process was guided both by our understanding of turning points reported by earlier authors and also by our expectation that long distance relationships may be subjected to unique forces of change. An initial set of categories was created by the first author based on a review of a sample of the interviews. In an effort to enhance the representativeness and clarity of the categories, their definitions and examples were discussed with the second author and with several grandparents. This process resulted in refined definitions for several categories and the collapsing of several categories deemed to be similar in meaning. The first author then reapplied the category system to the whole data set as she looked closely for negative cases. None were found. Theoretical saturation appeared to be reached after roughly 75% of the data were examined, but the whole data set was coded.

The process of identifying types of relational trajectories was similar. Each of the 30 graphs was reviewed and categorized with others that displayed a similar profile of changes in closeness, such as a marked and steady decline or a turbulent pattern of increased and decreased closeness. In this way, the process captured both similarities and differences in the kinds of relational changes reported by participants.

Discussion question 09

What do the turning-point counts represent?

The results identify 100 unique turning points: 50 positive and 50 negative across eight categories. Individuals reported between zero and 10, and eight grandparents reported none. The equal positive and negative counts describe events, rather than equal numbers of happy and unhappy people. They should not be interpreted as population prevalence or as 100 independent participants.

Discussion question 10

How did shared time, family dynamics, and geographic distance differ?

30-second answer

Spending Time Together was the most frequently cited category, and its reported events were unanimously positive, including visits, vacations, and holidays. Family Relational Dynamics included conflict, abuse, and living-arrangement changes; 12 events were negative, but some were positive. Geographic Distance described moves and reduced opportunities to meet as negative turning points. These are grandparents' interpretations of events, rather than experimental effects.

Evidence: TURNING-001

Read the source passage

The most cited and unanimously positive turning point was Spending Time Together, which primarily included interaction with a grandchild, visits, family vacations, or holiday gatherings. The driving force behind the increase in closeness in this category is undoubtedly the quality of interaction between a participant and their grandchild, as one participant describes spending time with her 15-year-old granddaughter:

It's starting to come up now (the level of closeness) because like I said, she comes down here the last three years, and she comes down here once a year, just alone, so she has a terrific time just for her and no peripheral involvement with other family members.

Other participants also identified this quality time as a symbol of being a part of a larger family unit. One participant implies that spending time with her 15-year-old grandson during his childhood allowed her to feel closer: “I'd stay at the house or mom and dad would go out, we spent more time together, and time together was the factor that made us closer.”

Family Relational Dynamics includes many instances of family conflict, and unexpected shifts within the family. Although the majority of these instances decreased relational closeness (N = 12), some family-oriented issues worked to bring participants closer to their grandchildren. As one participant explains, the abuse of his 15-year-old granddaughter shifted their relationship in a positive way.

Our granddaughter, among other things, was being abused emotionally by her father, uh, and needed protection and that was from the time she was a baby… so that was followed by a close period of helping her deal with and discover that her father was a son of a bitch, sick, sick, crazy. Mentally sick.

Another family-embedded circumstance that fell into this category is that of cohabitation. One participant acknowledged that a few months of living with her now 18-year-old granddaughter was not a positive foundation for their relationship.

I had hoped it would be a bonding experience, but I also found, in retrospect, I became the disciplinarian because my daughter wasn't there… by the time those 8 months were over, there was a lot of tension and stress between the three of us.

Consistent with previous research, (i.e. Holladay et al., 1998) Geographic Distance was cited quite often and was considered to be a negative turning point for participants. One participant indicates that her bond with her 16-year-old granddaughter weakened as a result of moving.

Let's see, the waning of the closeness was not any fault of the parent or the relationship, but rather the fact that she always lived in the same town until she was 6 and through divorce she moved to the eastern part of the states so I didn't get to see her as much…so we are close, just not as close as we would have been if we had been in the same town.

Another participant articulates a similar experience with her 15-year-old grandson: “But as they moved a further distance away, I got busier, both of his parents were working so we just lost some of that real closeness.”

We get to see her maybe once or twice a year, and every time we see her, it's the beginning of a new relationship, it's starting all over again, and I'm thrilled with some of the development that I see, but I am disappointed that I have very little to do with it.

What do the long-drive example and technology accounts illustrate?

A grandfather who drove 400 miles described disappointment when his granddaughter left after dinner to meet friends. The authors classified this as Lack of Relational Investment: he experienced the effort as one-sided, rather than describing distance alone. Read the source passage

Lack of Relational Investment is cited frequently. One participant illustrated this conundrum as he describes his frustration with his 19-year-old granddaughter's lack of interest in their relationship:

We'll come for Thanksgiving, she'll have dinner with us and then she'll say ‘I gotta go meet my friends’. Discussion question 14

How does the discussion interpret both change and stability?

30-second answer

About one-third reported marked decline, but eight grandparents perceived no change, so the study did not support inevitable deterioration during adolescence. Others appeared to maintain stability through visits and communication efforts. The discussion refers to more than 100 change events from 22 participants, while the results count 100 unique turning points; I would retain that wording distinction rather than present an exactly reconciled event count.

Evidence: DISCUSSION-001

Read the source passage

The results of this study contribute to the growing literature on long distance grandparenting relationships. They do so by identifying several distinct change trajectories reported by grandparents as well as positive and negative turning points in their relationships with adolescent grandchildren. As have other researchers (e.g., Holladay et al., 1998) we found the turning points methodology to be useful in generating qualitative data regarding changes in relational closeness. The reports of just 22 participants yielded more than 100 substantive change events—a rich set of insights about the circumstances that help and hinder grandparenting bonds. At the same time, eight grandparents perceived no changes in closeness, indicating that the turning point assumptions may not apply universally, a point explored recently by other researchers (Corman, Noonan, Reichman, & Schwartz-Soicher, 2011). These results imply that factors contributing to both stability and change should be examined in studies of this type.

Our analysis of relational trajectories suggested more variety in relational paths than might be expected from previous literature. About one-third of the long distance grandparents observed a marked decline as the grandchild developed into adolescence. This decline has been attributed to the child's growing independence and engagement with an expanding social network of peers and adults (Harwood, 2007). Our results indicate that the growing social networks and activity demands on adolescents make it more difficult (and perhaps reduce the motivation) to invest effort in maintaining the relationship. Identity affirmation and other relational rewards previously conveyed by the grandparent may now be available from other sources. At the same time, our participants reported life changes such as retirement or illness during this period. Consistent with previous work (Holladay et al., 1998), grandparents in our study reported moving away during the adolescent years or reported their own engagements in new activities. In this way, the trend toward declining closeness appears to be exacerbated by life changes of both parties.

Our data failed to support the idea that relational closeness inevitably declines over time. Indeed, interviewees often reported stability, even during the grandchild's adolescence. Some grandparents simply perceived no change over time. In at least one case, low relational closeness reflected the grandparent's limited involvement in family life from the grandchild's earliest years. But other grandparents seemed to cultivate stability through an intentional effort to engage with the adolescent child. They did so by scheduling visits or adopting new forms of communication, such as texting, social media, or Skype. This finding is consistent with that of Holladay and Seipke (2007) who suggested that grandparents remain relatively satisfied with long distance relationships, in part by substituting telephone conversations for personal contacts with the grandchild. Our data suggest that continued relational investment by either party, or the lack thereof, is perceived as a relational turning point.

Discussion question 15

What are the main limitations and practical implications?

30-second answer

Closeness was subjective, cultural diversity was limited, and retrospective trajectories began at age five; some grandchildren had not completed adolescence. The authors propose arranging shared time, learning communication technologies, adjusting expectations, and sustaining involvement during family disruption. These are practical implications from qualitative accounts, rather than tested interventions, and the partial trajectories do not establish universal lifelong patterns.

Evidence: LIMITATIONS-001

Read the source passage

Results of this study must be interpreted with some caution. One limitation stems from the highly subjective nature of relational closeness. Although a key component of the turning point methodology involved clarification of the term “closeness”, grandparents may apply the term differently. Some long distance grandparents seemed to have been quite uninvolved (by choice or circumstance) in their grandchildren's lives from the very beginning; a result is that they perceived no turning points. Another limitation involves the sample, which is limited in cultural diversity. Future research should examine long distance grandparenting in cultural contexts, such as the traditional Hispanic family, where geographic separation is likely to be unsettling, stigmatized, and challenging. Finally, the relational trajectories reported here are partial. Some of the grandchildren had yet to complete adolescence. We did not track turning points before the age of five. Thus we may have missed some of the early or later life events that shape the closeness of relationships of this type.

Several avenues of additional research present themselves. First, the use of emerging technology in long-distance grandparenting relationships is a rich area of inquiry. The extent to which relationships can be enhanced by photo-sharing, Skype, and other tools has yet to be fully explored. Another concern is the role of relational expectancies. It appeared that negative turning points were often the result of unfulfilled expectations about spending time with a grandchild. It may be that expectancy match is more important than any particular relational practice. Finally, cultural differences in long distance grandparenting need to be understood. How do expectations and communication practices vary across ethnic groups, particularly when cultural norms support strong social ties among extended family members, including adolescents and their grandparents?

Our results have practical implications for grandparents and those who work with them. First, grandparents may be able to increase or stabilize closeness in some relationships. Finding ways to spend time with distant grandchildren (e.g. scheduling visits or sharing a vacation) is one of those. Learning to use emerging technology is another positive step, one that might be particularly important for grandparents living at a distance. Memories can be sources of strength. Grandparents who recalled the adolescent years of their own children seemed better able to weather the changes of adolescent grandchildren. Changed expectations may be helpful as the grandchild ages; grandparents who tempered their expectations about the adolescent's investment of time and effort seemed to experience less disappointment. Finally, the largely negative impact of disruption of the family system should be of interest to those who counsel families. Contrary to some prior research, the grandparent and grandchild tended not to draw closer during family crises, perhaps due to the complications of distance. Finding ways to keep these grandparents involved and supportive of adolescents during times of family distress might be a point of discussion during family therapy.

This study examines changes in long-distance relationships between grandparents and their adolescent grandchildren by identifying relational turning points and trajectories. Qualitative analysis of data collected from interviews with grandparents yielded 100 unique turning points. Constant comparative analysis revealed eight distinct categories of relational turning points; Spending Time Together, Family Relational Dynamics, Geographic Distance, Lack of Relational Investment, Use of Technology, Relational Investment, Lack of Free Time, and Grandchild Gaining Independence. These varied in the degree to which they positively or negatively impacted relational closeness. Application of the Retrospective Interview Technique (RIT) yielded five distinctive relational trajectories: Decrease in Closeness, Increase in Closeness, Multidimensional Changes in Closeness, Minimal Changes in Closeness, and Consistent Relational Closeness. The results expose the communicative challenges faced by long-distance-grandparents, the diversity of these relationships, and the ways in which grandparenting bonds change over time. Implications for an enriched understanding of grandparenting relationships and practical applications for families are explored.

How did the paper change your interpretation of reduced contact?

30-second answer

I was struck by how the perceived reason for less contact mattered. Unreciprocated effort could leave grandparents feeling shut out and make the relationship seem one-sided. Evidence: DISCUSSION-003

Read the source passage

Family Relational Dynamics was a complicated theme, including instances of parental divorce, changes in living arrangements, abuse, and other shifts in the ecology of the family system. Previously Soliz (2008) argued that grandparents can be important sources of support to adolescents during divorce. Our own results are less encouraging, or at least ambivalent in this regard. In some cases, such as an instance when a grandmother shared living space with the grandchild and recently divorced mother, the opportunities for new companionship and routine conversation were viewed as a positive turning point. But more often, family complications were barriers to closeness, as when a divorce resulted in one of the parents barring contact with grandparents from the “opposite side” of the family. In other cases, grandparents became estranged from their own children, due to conflicts over financial support or drug abuse. It may be that long distance grandparents, compared to those who are geographically present, are particularly disadvantaged by these family disruptions.

A unanimously negative turning point category, Lack of Relational Investment, displayed a general feeling of disinterest in the relationship from either the grandparent or the grandchild. Within this category, participants often characterized the relationship as “one sided”, indicating that their efforts to communicate through letters, phone calls, or inviting their grandchild to visit are not reciprocated by their adolescent grandchildren. The symbolic importance of reciprocation to grandparents has been noted elsewhere (Harwood, 2000; Holladay & Seipke, 2007). It seems that these experiences left participants feeling psychologically “shut out” by their grandchildren and in some cases undermined their desire to invest in the relationship. Although also a negative turning point, Lack of Free Time was generalized as less detrimental to the relationship. A grandchild having limited time to spend with a grandparent was often seen as a necessary component of child development which participants generally did not take personally. The impact on closeness, although negative, was not as substantial. This is perhaps because participants were more accepting of a grandchild's inability to make significant relational investments as a result of being involved in many activities.

How would you read the technology finding critically?

Evidence: DISCUSSION-002

Read the source passage

When closeness ebbed during adolescence, grandparents reported making adjustments, such as planning a visit or initiating contact through social media. Apparently, long-distance bonds, similar to face-to-face relationships (see Mansson, Myers, & Turner, 2010), can be steadied when the parties engage actively in relationship maintenance practices. This finding may be particularly heartening for grandparents who wonder how the relationship might be sustained through the potentially challenging adolescent period. It is worth noting, however, that a small but notable portion of these relationships experienced more dramatic changes. As observed by others, (e.g., Holladay et al., 1998) these changes were sometimes attributable to major disruptions in family life, such as death, illness or divorce of the parents.

Taken together, the results pertaining to RQ1 tell us that long distance grandparents might expect some decline in the closeness of their relationships with adolescent grandchildren. However, they also suggest that geographic distance is not the relational barrier it appeared to be at an earlier time (e.g., Harwood, 2000a). The last decade has seen a surge in the use of cell phones and social media platforms, particularly by adolescents. In some ways, technology has created opportunities for grandparents to communicate directly with grandchildren and to stay abreast of their lives. In our interviews, it appeared that grandparents who make use of these technologies are more likely to sustain close relationships over time.

The second research question directed our attention to the types of turning points reported by long distance grandparents. Several of these were consistent with those reported in previous studies. Two turning points, Spending Time Together and Geographic Distance, were similar to themes reported in the Holladay et al. (1998) study of granddaughters. Shared time is harder to come by when the parties live at a distance, so efforts to overcome the barrier of distance seemed to carry considerable symbolic importance. Of course, the mere physical presence of a family member creates opportunities for self-disclosure, mutual affirmation, and other communication practices that facilitate closeness.

Finding ways to keep these grandparents involved and supportive of adolescents during times of family distress might be a point of discussion during family therapy.
