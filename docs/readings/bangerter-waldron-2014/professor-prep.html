Geographic distance and changing closeness거리와 친밀감의 변화

What interested you about closeness in long-distance grandparenting?장거리 관계에서 무엇이 흥미로웠나요?

첫 답변

I found it interesting that living far apart did not mean every relationship grew less close along the same path. Grandparents recalled five patterns, including decline, stability, fluctuations, and increase. Stability did not always mean a close bond; some relationships had remained distant from the beginning. I would therefore distinguish where people lived from how close they felt and ask how that feeling changed over time. These were grandparents' recollections of the relationship.멀리 산다고 모든 관계가 똑같이 소원해지는 것은 아니라는 점이 흥미로웠습니다. 조부모가 회상한 관계에는 감소뿐 아니라 유지, 오르내림, 증가 등 다섯 가지 궤적이 있었습니다. 변화가 없다는 말도 늘 친했다는 뜻은 아니어서, 처음부터 소원한 관계도 포함됐습니다. 그래서 사는 곳의 거리와 느끼는 친밀감을 나누고, 그 감정이 시간에 따라 어떻게 달라졌는지 봐야겠다고 생각했습니다. 자료가 조부모의 회상이라는 점도 기억하려 합니다.

Does that mean geographic distance did not matter?그렇다면 사는 곳의 거리는 무관한가요?

No. Geographic Distance accounted for 12 of the 100 turning points, and all 12 were classified as negative. Participants described fewer opportunities to see their grandchildren after a move, sometimes feeling that each visit meant starting the relationship again. The distinction is that distance could be a difficulty without determining every relationship's entire course. The study included long-distance relationships rather than a matched nearby comparison group, and these were grandparents' interpretations of events. It therefore does not estimate an independent causal effect of distance or show that distance never matters.그렇게 읽지는 않았습니다. 전환점 100건 중 지리적 거리 범주는 12건이었고, 모두 부정적으로 분류됐습니다. 이사 뒤 만날 기회가 줄거나, 만날 때마다 관계를 처음부터 다시 시작하는 듯하다는 이야기도 나왔습니다. 거리가 어려움이 될 수 있다는 사실과 모든 관계의 전체 경로를 결정한다는 주장은 구분해야 합니다. 가까이 사는 비교집단을 따로 둔 연구가 아니라 장거리 관계에 대한 조부모의 해석을 모은 연구이므로, 거리만의 인과효과를 추정하거나 거리가 무관하다고 결론 내릴 수는 없습니다.

근거: TURNING-001

원문 근거 읽기

The most cited and unanimously positive turning point was Spending Time Together, which primarily included interaction with a grandchild, visits, family vacations, or holiday gatherings. The driving force behind the increase in closeness in this category is undoubtedly the quality of interaction between a participant and their grandchild, as one participant describes spending time with her 15-year-old granddaughter:

It's starting to come up now (the level of closeness) because like I said, she comes down here the last three years, and she comes down here once a year, just alone, so she has a terrific time just for her and no peripheral involvement with other family members.

Other participants also identified this quality time as a symbol of being a part of a larger family unit. One participant implies that spending time with her 15-year-old grandson during his childhood allowed her to feel closer: “I'd stay at the house or mom and dad would go out, we spent more time together, and time together was the factor that made us closer.”

Family Relational Dynamics includes many instances of family conflict, and unexpected shifts within the family. Although the majority of these instances decreased relational closeness (N = 12), some family-oriented issues worked to bring participants closer to their grandchildren. As one participant explains, the abuse of his 15-year-old granddaughter shifted their relationship in a positive way.

Our granddaughter, among other things, was being abused emotionally by her father, uh, and needed protection and that was from the time she was a baby… so that was followed by a close period of helping her deal with and discover that her father was a son of a bitch, sick, sick, crazy. Mentally sick.

Another family-embedded circumstance that fell into this category is that of cohabitation. One participant acknowledged that a few months of living with her now 18-year-old granddaughter was not a positive foundation for their relationship.

I had hoped it would be a bonding experience, but I also found, in retrospect, I became the disciplinarian because my daughter wasn't there… by the time those 8 months were over, there was a lot of tension and stress between the three of us.

Consistent with previous research, (i.e. Holladay et al., 1998) Geographic Distance was cited quite often and was considered to be a negative turning point for participants. One participant indicates that her bond with her 16-year-old granddaughter weakened as a result of moving.

Let's see, the waning of the closeness was not any fault of the parent or the relationship, but rather the fact that she always lived in the same town until she was 6 and through divorce she moved to the eastern part of the states so I didn't get to see her as much…so we are close, just not as close as we would have been if we had been in the same town.

Another participant articulates a similar experience with her 15-year-old grandson: “But as they moved a further distance away, I got busier, both of his parents were working so we just lost some of that real closeness.”

Other participants expressed a more severe depletion of closeness. One grandfather indicates that the geographic distance between him and his 12-year-old granddaughter has deterred him from cultivating an involved relationship with her.

We get to see her maybe once or twice a year, and every time we see her, it's the beginning of a new relationship, it's starting all over again, and I'm thrilled with some of the development that I see, but I am disappointed that I have very little to do with it.

What do the five trajectories tell us about stability and change?다섯 궤적은 서로 어떻게 달랐나요?

The 30 graphs were grouped into marked decrease, 10; consistent closeness, 8; minimal changes, 5; multidimensional changes, 5; and marked increase, 2. The consistent group included both consistently close and consistently distant relationships, so a flat line describes a lack of change, not necessarily a strong bond. The minimal-change group retained high closeness despite small shifts, whereas the multidimensional group showed larger fluctuations and varied endpoints. These are types found in this sample. They do not give every grandparent-grandchild relationship a fixed developmental sequence or supply population probabilities for what happens next.30개의 그래프는 뚜렷한 감소 10명, 일정한 친밀감 8명, 작은 변화 5명, 여러 방향의 큰 변화 5명, 뚜렷한 증가 2명으로 묶였습니다. 일정한 집단에는 계속 친한 관계와 계속 소원한 관계가 모두 있어서, 평평한 선은 변화가 없다는 뜻이지 반드시 유대가 강하다는 뜻은 아닙니다. 작은 변화 집단은 소폭 오르내리면서도 높은 친밀감을 유지했고, 큰 변화 집단은 변동과 최종 수준이 다양했습니다. 이 표본에서 나온 유형이므로 누구나 같은 단계를 거친다거나 다음 변화의 확률을 보여 준다고 해석하지 않겠습니다.

Were those changes measured repeatedly over the years?그 변화는 여러 해 반복 측정했나요?

They were reconstructed retrospectively. Researchers interviewed 35 grandparents; five interviews could not be transcribed, leaving 30 for analysis. Each grandparent selected one grandchild currently aged 12–19, recalled closeness from age five to the grandchild's current age, and drew a graph while discussing turning points. Closeness meant the psychological bond the grandparent felt toward the grandchild. The age axis therefore organizes remembered time, rather than repeated measurements collected at each age. The study makes grandparents' views of change visible, but does not independently establish what the grandchild felt then or verify a causal sequence through longitudinal observation.여러 해에 걸쳐 반복 측정한 것이 아니라 회상으로 재구성했습니다. 조부모 35명을 면담했지만 5명의 면담은 전사할 수 없어 30명을 분석했습니다. 각 조부모가 현재 12~19세인 손자녀 한 명을 골라, 그 아이의 5세부터 현재까지 친밀감을 떠올려 그래프를 그리고 전환점을 설명했습니다. 친밀감은 조부모가 손자녀에게 느끼는 심리적 유대로 정의했습니다. 연령축은 기억 속 시간을 정리한 것이므로, 각 나이에 실제로 조사한 종단자료와는 다릅니다. 조부모가 변화를 어떻게 이해하는지는 보여 주지만, 당시 손자녀의 감정이나 사건의 인과관계를 독립적으로 확인한 자료는 아닙니다.

근거: PROCEDURE-001

원문 근거 읽기

Turning points were generated using the Retrospective Interview Technique (RIT), in which informants are interviewed about turning points that have taken place within the relationship. Participants were then asked to graph those turning points on a grid in order to generate an understanding of how the relationship has changed over time and what events caused these changes (Baxter & Pittman, 2001). RIT is a particularly interactive methodology that allows the participant and the researcher to gain a visual representation of the relationship and is especially suitable for building participant/researcher rapport. Thus, this strategy is appropriate for gaining a valid understanding of relational events that shape the closeness of grandparents and their grandchildren and allows these events to be understood from a developmental perspective.

The term ‘closeness’ was isolated and discussed at great length in order to ensure that participants understood this important aspect of the study. Consistent with Golish (2000), closeness was defined as the psychological bond that a grandparent feels towards the grandchild. Once it was evident that participants understood the definition of relational closeness, the interview shifted to discuss and define turning points that cause changes in relational closeness. The theoretical concept of relational turning points was thoroughly discussed and participants were given several examples of turning points cited from previous research studies (e.g. Holladay et al., 1998) such as a death in the family, marriage, moving out of state, or the birth of a child. Defining these terms early in the interview process ensured that participants were able to think about these relationships in ways that correlate to the purpose of the research study.

Next, participants were asked to select one of their long-distance grandchildren to discuss during the interview. They were then presented with a RIT graph that included the level of closeness on the y-axis (1 — most close; 5 — least close) and the age of the child on the x-axis. Participants plotted changes in relational closeness beginning at age five until the grandchild's current age. Beginning the study of relational closeness at age five was justified not only by similar retrospective research, (Golish, 2000) but also by Piaget's theory of the preoperational stage of cognitive development. At the preoperational stage, which occurs around age five, a child produces mental abilities that are essential to relational development (Miller & Church, 2003). The interview then transitioned to a discussion of each turning point. Interviews ranged from approximately 7 min to 45 min in length. Immediately following the interview, a transcript was made from the recording, yielding 116 pages of double-spaced text. The thirty hand-drawn RIT graphs were converted to digital displays (see Figs. 1–5).

Contact and the meaning of relational effort연락에 담긴 관계의 의미

What interested you about how grandparents and grandchildren kept in touch?연락 방식에서 무엇이 흥미로웠나요?

첫 답변

I found it interesting that keeping in touch involved both using a means of communication and feeling that an interest in the relationship was returned. Grandparents described texting and video calls that brought them into their grandchildren's everyday lives as positive turning points, while feeling that they alone made an effort was a negative turning point. I would therefore look at what people talk about and how their attempts to connect are received, alongside how often they contact each other.연락 수단을 쓰는 것뿐 아니라, 관계에 대한 관심이 돌아온다고 느끼는지가 중요하다는 점이 흥미로웠습니다. 조부모들은 문자나 영상통화로 손자녀의 일상에 참여한 일을 긍정적인 전환점으로, 자신만 애쓴다고 느낀 일을 부정적인 전환점으로 말했습니다. 그래서 연락 횟수만 세기보다, 어떤 이야기를 나누고 관계를 이어 가려는 노력이 어떻게 받아들여지는지 함께 살펴보고 싶습니다.

Lack of Relational Investment is cited frequently. One participant illustrated this conundrum as he describes his frustration with his 19-year-old granddaughter's lack of interest in their relationship:

We'll come for Thanksgiving, she'll have dinner with us and then she'll say ‘I gotta go meet my friends’. Well, your grandparents just drove 400 miles, we haven't seen you since whenever, would it be so wrong for us to expect that you could stay for a little bit longer after dinner and inquire about our life…So I don't know if it's disappointment, or if it's anger, but you find there is a certain upset that you feel, there's like a disenfranchisement, which may be more mental than real, and the relationship begins to feel too one sided, the effort seems to be one sided.

This participant's feelings are echoed throughout the turning points within this category, like a grandmother of an 18-year-old granddaughter who shares a similar frustration:

But there's no connection between us anymore, I have visited them many times and she's either with her friends or drawing a picture or playing with one of her electronic toys, she doesn't seem to want to spend time talking, she doesn't seem interested in me or my life.

Use of Technology, exemplifies the role that modern forms of communication may play in strengthening these relationships and adds to previous studies that considered older technologies like email and landlines (i.e. Harwood, 2000a; Holladay & Seipke, 2007). This category was dominantly positive. One grandfather indicates that texting has become an important part of his relationship with his 15-year-old granddaughter: “Technology is one thing, my granddaughter currently has a cell phone so she is able to use her phone to call and she is able to use her phone to text and I can text back.” Another participant recalls how her relationship with her 17-year-old granddaughter opened up as a result of text-messaging. “And she said ‘grandma, do you text?’ and I said ‘yes, let's text!’”

This category also includes social media, which served as a way for grandparents to feel more connected to their grandchildren. A grandmother spoke of how her relationship with her 13-year-old granddaughter (and 14-year-old granddaughter) has been enriched since becoming ‘Friends’ on Facebook:

You know, it's so much fun for me, and what I do, I usually keep my mouth shut, they know I'm there and so far they're still comfortable with it and you know, I never interfere with their relationships with their friends or anything, but it's so fun to see things they do, and I mean if I got on the phone with them and tried to have them tell me some of the things they do, it wouldn't happen.

Another participant spoke about how Skype allowed her to re-connect with her 13-year-old granddaughter:

Basically what she did was, well I hadn't seen her for 8 months, 7 months maybe, and she just put the computer on her bed and I said ‘oh that looks like a new poster on your wall’ and so we kind of did this little virtual tour of her room and it was just like she and I were hanging out, no one else in the house even knew we were talking, her door was shut to her room. I was there on the computer, and it was really cool.

Family Relational Dynamics was a complicated theme, including instances of parental divorce, changes in living arrangements, abuse, and other shifts in the ecology of the family system. The symbolic importance of reciprocation to grandparents has been noted elsewhere (Harwood, 2000; Holladay & Seipke, 2007). It seems that these experiences left participants feeling psychologically “shut out” by their grandchildren and in some cases undermined their desire to invest in the relationship. Although also a negative turning point, Lack of Free Time was generalized as less detrimental to the relationship. A grandchild having limited time to spend with a grandparent was often seen as a necessary component of child development which participants generally did not take personally. The impact on closeness, although negative, was not as substantial. This is perhaps because participants were more accepting of a grandchild's inability to make significant relational investments as a result of being involved in many activities.

근거: PROCEDURE-001

원문 근거 읽기

Turning points were generated using the Retrospective Interview Technique (RIT), in which informants are interviewed about turning points that have taken place within the relationship. Participants were then asked to graph those turning points on a grid in order to generate an understanding of how the relationship has changed over time and what events caused these changes (Baxter & Pittman, 2001). RIT is a particularly interactive methodology that allows the participant and the researcher to gain a visual representation of the relationship and is especially suitable for building participant/researcher rapport. Thus, this strategy is appropriate for gaining a valid understanding of relational events that shape the closeness of grandparents and their grandchildren and allows these events to be understood from a developmental perspective.

The term ‘closeness’ was isolated and discussed at great length in order to ensure that participants understood this important aspect of the study. Consistent with Golish (2000), closeness was defined as the psychological bond that a grandparent feels towards the grandchild. Once it was evident that participants understood the definition of relational closeness, the interview shifted to discuss and define turning points that cause changes in relational closeness. The theoretical concept of relational turning points was thoroughly discussed and participants were given several examples of turning points cited from previous research studies (e.g. Holladay et al., 1998) such as a death in the family, marriage, moving out of state, or the birth of a child. Defining these terms early in the interview process ensured that participants were able to think about these relationships in ways that correlate to the purpose of the research study.

Next, participants were asked to select one of their long-distance grandchildren to discuss during the interview. They were then presented with a RIT graph that included the level of closeness on the y-axis (1 — most close; 5 — least close) and the age of the child on the x-axis. Participants plotted changes in relational closeness beginning at age five until the grandchild's current age. Beginning the study of relational closeness at age five was justified not only by similar retrospective research, (Golish, 2000) but also by Piaget's theory of the preoperational stage of cognitive development. At the preoperational stage, which occurs around age five, a child produces mental abilities that are essential to relational development (Miller & Church, 2003). The interview then transitioned to a discussion of each turning point. Interviews ranged from approximately 7 min to 45 min in length. Immediately following the interview, a transcript was made from the recording, yielding 116 pages of double-spaced text. The thirty hand-drawn RIT graphs were converted to digital displays (see Figs. 1–5).

근거: LIMITATIONS-001

원문 근거 읽기

Results of this study must be interpreted with some caution. One limitation stems from the highly subjective nature of relational closeness. Although a key component of the turning point methodology involved clarification of the term “closeness”, grandparents may apply the term differently. Some long distance grandparents seemed to have been quite uninvolved (by choice or circumstance) in their grandchildren's lives from the very beginning; a result is that they perceived no turning points. Another limitation involves the sample, which is limited in cultural diversity. Future research should examine long distance grandparenting in cultural contexts, such as the traditional Hispanic family, where geographic separation is likely to be unsettling, stigmatized, and challenging. Finally, the relational trajectories reported here are partial. Some of the grandchildren had yet to complete adolescence. We did not track turning points before the age of five. Thus we may have missed some of the early or later life events that shape the closeness of relationships of this type.

Several avenues of additional research present themselves. First, the use of emerging technology in long-distance grandparenting relationships is a rich area of inquiry. The extent to which relationships can be enhanced by photo-sharing, Skype, and other tools has yet to be fully explored. Another concern is the role of relational expectancies. It appeared that negative turning points were often the result of unfulfilled expectations about spending time with a grandchild. It may be that expectancy match is more important than any particular relational practice. Finally, cultural differences in long distance grandparenting need to be understood. How do expectations and communication practices vary across ethnic groups, particularly when cultural norms support strong social ties among extended family members, including adolescents and their grandparents?

Our results have practical implications for grandparents and those who work with them. First, grandparents may be able to increase or stabilize closeness in some relationships. Finding ways to spend time with distant grandchildren (e.g. scheduling visits or sharing a vacation) is one of those. Learning to use emerging technology is another positive step, one that might be particularly important for grandparents living at a distance. Memories can be sources of strength. Grandparents who recalled the adolescent years of their own children seemed better able to weather the changes of adolescent grandchildren. Changed expectations may be helpful as the grandchild ages; grandparents who tempered their expectations about the adolescent's investment of time and effort seemed to experience less disappointment. Finally, the largely negative impact of disruption of the family system should be of interest to those who counsel families. Contrary to some prior research, the grandparent and grandchild tended not to draw closer during family crises, perhaps due to the complications of distance. Finding ways to keep these grandparents involved and supportive of adolescents during times of family distress might be a point of discussion during family therapy.

근거: LIMITATIONS-001

원문 근거 읽기

Results of this study must be interpreted with some caution. One limitation stems from the highly subjective nature of relational closeness. Although a key component of the turning point methodology involved clarification of the term “closeness”, grandparents may apply the term differently. Some long distance grandparents seemed to have been quite uninvolved (by choice or circumstance) in their grandchildren's lives from the very beginning; a result is that they perceived no turning points. Another limitation involves the sample, which is limited in cultural diversity. Future research should examine long distance grandparenting in cultural contexts, such as the traditional Hispanic family, where geographic separation is likely to be unsettling, stigmatized, and challenging. Finally, the relational trajectories reported here are partial. Some of the grandchildren had yet to complete adolescence. We did not track turning points before the age of five. Thus we may have missed some of the early or later life events that shape the closeness of relationships of this type.

Several avenues of additional research present themselves. First, the use of emerging technology in long-distance grandparenting relationships is a rich area of inquiry. The extent to which relationships can be enhanced by photo-sharing, Skype, and other tools has yet to be fully explored. Another concern is the role of relational expectancies. It appeared that negative turning points were often the result of unfulfilled expectations about spending time with a grandchild. It may be that expectancy match is more important than any particular relational practice. Finally, cultural differences in long distance grandparenting need to be understood. How do expectations and communication practices vary across ethnic groups, particularly when cultural norms support strong social ties among extended family members, including adolescents and their grandparents?

Our results have practical implications for grandparents and those who work with them. First, grandparents may be able to increase or stabilize closeness in some relationships. Finding ways to spend time with distant grandchildren (e.g. scheduling visits or sharing a vacation) is one of those. Learning to use emerging technology is another positive step, one that might be particularly important for grandparents living at a distance. Memories can be sources of strength. Grandparents who recalled the adolescent years of their own children seemed better able to weather the changes of adolescent grandchildren. Changed expectations may be helpful as the grandchild ages; grandparents who tempered their expectations about the adolescent's investment of time and effort seemed to experience less disappointment. Finally, the largely negative impact of disruption of the family system should be of interest to those who counsel families. Contrary to some prior research, the grandparent and grandchild tended not to draw closer during family crises, perhaps due to the complications of distance. Finding ways to keep these grandparents involved and supportive of adolescents during times of family distress might be a point of discussion during family therapy.

근거: PROCEDURE-001

원문 근거 읽기

Turning points were generated using the Retrospective Interview Technique (RIT), in which informants are interviewed about turning points that have taken place within the relationship. Participants were then asked to graph those turning points on a grid in order to generate an understanding of how the relationship has changed over time and what events caused these changes (Baxter & Pittman, 2001). RIT is a particularly interactive methodology that allows the participant and the researcher to gain a visual representation of the relationship and is especially suitable for building participant/researcher rapport. Thus, this strategy is appropriate for gaining a valid understanding of relational events that shape the closeness of grandparents and their grandchildren and allows these events to be understood from a developmental perspective.

The term ‘closeness’ was isolated and discussed at great length in order to ensure that participants understood this important aspect of the study. Consistent with Golish (2000), closeness was defined as the psychological bond that a grandparent feels towards the grandchild. Once it was evident that participants understood the definition of relational closeness, the interview shifted to discuss and define turning points that cause changes in relational closeness. The theoretical concept of relational turning points was thoroughly discussed and participants were given several examples of turning points cited from previous research studies (e.g. Holladay et al., 1998) such as a death in the family, marriage, moving out of state, or the birth of a child. Defining these terms early in the interview process ensured that participants were able to think about these relationships in ways that correlate to the purpose of the research study.

Next, participants were asked to select one of their long-distance grandchildren to discuss during the interview. They were then presented with a RIT graph that included the level of closeness on the y-axis (1 — most close; 5 — least close) and the age of the child on the x-axis. Participants plotted changes in relational closeness beginning at age five until the grandchild's current age. Beginning the study of relational closeness at age five was justified not only by similar retrospective research, (Golish, 2000) but also by Piaget's theory of the preoperational stage of cognitive development. At the preoperational stage, which occurs around age five, a child produces mental abilities that are essential to relational development (Miller & Church, 2003). The interview then transitioned to a discussion of each turning point. Interviews ranged from approximately 7 min to 45 min in length. Immediately following the interview, a transcript was made from the recording, yielding 116 pages of double-spaced text. The thirty hand-drawn RIT graphs were converted to digital displays (see Figs. 1–5).

근거: LIMITATIONS-001

원문 근거 읽기

Results of this study must be interpreted with some caution. One limitation stems from the highly subjective nature of relational closeness. Although a key component of the turning point methodology involved clarification of the term “closeness”, grandparents may apply the term differently. Some long distance grandparents seemed to have been quite uninvolved (by choice or circumstance) in their grandchildren's lives from the very beginning; a result is that they perceived no turning points. Another limitation involves the sample, which is limited in cultural diversity. Future research should examine long distance grandparenting in cultural contexts, such as the traditional Hispanic family, where geographic separation is likely to be unsettling, stigmatized, and challenging. Finally, the relational trajectories reported here are partial. Some of the grandchildren had yet to complete adolescence. We did not track turning points before the age of five. Thus we may have missed some of the early or later life events that shape the closeness of relationships of this type.

Several avenues of additional research present themselves. First, the use of emerging technology in long-distance grandparenting relationships is a rich area of inquiry. The extent to which relationships can be enhanced by photo-sharing, Skype, and other tools has yet to be fully explored. Another concern is the role of relational expectancies. It appeared that negative turning points were often the result of unfulfilled expectations about spending time with a grandchild. It may be that expectancy match is more important than any particular relational practice. Finally, cultural differences in long distance grandparenting need to be understood. How do expectations and communication practices vary across ethnic groups, particularly when cultural norms support strong social ties among extended family members, including adolescents and their grandparents?

Our results have practical implications for grandparents and those who work with them. First, grandparents may be able to increase or stabilize closeness in some relationships. Finding ways to spend time with distant grandchildren (e.g. scheduling visits or sharing a vacation) is one of those. Learning to use emerging technology is another positive step, one that might be particularly important for grandparents living at a distance. Memories can be sources of strength. Grandparents who recalled the adolescent years of their own children seemed better able to weather the changes of adolescent grandchildren. Changed expectations may be helpful as the grandchild ages; grandparents who tempered their expectations about the adolescent's investment of time and effort seemed to experience less disappointment. Finally, the largely negative impact of disruption of the family system should be of interest to those who counsel families. Contrary to some prior research, the grandparent and grandchild tended not to draw closer during family crises, perhaps due to the complications of distance. Finding ways to keep these grandparents involved and supportive of adolescents during times of family distress might be a point of discussion during family therapy.

How would that change the way you support a family?그렇게 읽으면 가족을 지원하는 방식이 어떻게 달라질까요?

I would use the readings to guide a conversation about expectations and maintenance rather than prescribe more contact for everyone. Bangerter and Waldron distinguish perceived disinterest from limited free time and describe adjustments to communication and expectations during adolescence. Kalmijn and Leopold show why support around parental loss should consider the surviving parent and the longer period after both parents have died, when contact may fade. As an application, families could discuss which connections they value and how voluntary responsibilities for staying in touch might be shared. These are proposals, not tested interventions. Increased contact alone would not demonstrate improved closeness, and preferences, strained relationships, and unequal capacity to contribute would need attention.저는 모든 사람에게 더 자주 연락하라고 처방하기보다 기대와 관계 유지에 관한 대화를 이끄는 데 두 읽기를 활용하겠습니다. Bangerter와 Waldron은 지각된 무관심과 부족한 자유 시간을 구분하며 청소년기에 소통과 기대를 조정하는 방식을 설명합니다. Kalmijn과 Leopold는 부모 상실을 지원할 때 생존 부모와, 두 부모가 모두 사망해 접촉이 희미해질 수 있는 장기적인 시기를 고려해야 하는 이유를 보여 줍니다. 적용 방안으로 가족은 어떤 연결을 소중히 여기는지, 연락을 이어 가는 자발적인 책임을 어떻게 나눌지 이야기할 수 있습니다. 이는 검증된 개입이 아니라 제안입니다. 접촉 증가만으로 친밀감 향상을 입증할 수 없으며, 선호, 긴장된 관계, 기여할 수 있는 능력의 차이도 살펴야 합니다.
