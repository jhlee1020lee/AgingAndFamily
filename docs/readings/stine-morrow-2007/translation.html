초록

Cognitive aging is often characterized as a process in which two competing forces determine individual development: a genetically driven senescence process that engenders declines in mental mechanics; and an accumulation of life experience that augments cultural, pragmatic, and knowledge-based competence. The considerable variability in the level and rate of change in complex intellectual activities (e.g., language understanding) is often accounted for in terms of individual differences in abilities associated with these forces. I argue that choice in how effort is allocated may be an essential determinant of cognitive change over the life span--both directly, in the form of attentional engagement, and indirectly, as it sculpts neural substrates that give rise to component abilities.

The question raised in this article is whether individuals have some degree of control over these developmental trajectories. In some ways, as we age, we are put in much the same situation as the fictional wizard Harry Potter when he dons the “sorting hat” that selects the house to which he will be assigned at the wizarding school Hogwarts (Rowling, 1997). It is a hard decision for the sorting hat because Harry has certain characteristics of the noble wizards of the house of Gryffindor and the evil wizards of the house of Slytherin (who are also clever and decisive in difficult situations). Hearing the sorting hat’s murmurings, Harry asks not to be put into Slytherin, but into Gryffindor. The hat tries to convince him otherwise, but Harry insists, and the hat finally agrees. Similarly, as we grow older, we struggle with the “sorting hat” of cultural expectations about aging. When faced with intellectual challenges requiring great courage (e.g., keeping track of names at a party, learning new technology, or deciphering retirement plans), we wonder whether we are truly up to the task. We make (implicit and explicit) choices to seek out and engage cognitively challenging situations in meaningful and mindful ways or else to withdraw from activities--either because we buy into cultural stereotypes of diminished cognitive capacity or because opportunities for meaningful intellectual challenge are less available, or both. We often make this choice in cultural contexts in which intellectual capacity is viewed as a fixed entity that cannot be substantially altered through effort (Molden & Dweck, 2006) and in which older adults very typically do not expect to be able to control cognitive outcomes (Hess et al., 2003). To the extent that we do withdraw from intellectual activity, it is possible that performance deteriorates because task-relevant processes are not implemented, as well as because disuse may exaggerate senescence-related declines in cognitive function (which underpins performance).

자기조절, 관여, 인지 최적화

Choosing to engage in intellectual activity may be an important determinant of cognitive optimization in two very different senses. First, assuming that cognitive performance ultimately arises from the execution of cognitive operations that underpin that performance, outcomes will be enhanced if computations are conducted effectively (e.g., allocating effort to organizational and elaborative processing for good memory); we can call this implementation of task-specific processes attentional engagement. In other words, effective allocation of attentional resources is important in any cognitive task just to get the job done. The example of language processing will be used to illustrate this principle (Stine-Morrow, Miller, & Hertzog, 2006). Second, habitually selecting environmental contexts that are intellectually stimulating--or activity engagement--may potentially expand capacity (Kramer, Bherer, Colcombe, Dong, & Greenough, 2004). Thus, I propose that habitual self-regulation of one’s cognitive processing and one’s environmental context may contribute to intellectual vitality in later adulthood, both by augmenting performance and by sculpting the cognitive system that engenders future performance.

주의 관여

In spite of the fact that verbal ability is a crystallized ability that often shows resilience into old age, memory for spoken and written language often reveals declines with age (Johnson, 2003). In our lab, we have investigated the extent to which these deficits may be rooted in nonoptimal attentional engagement. A visual representation of our Self-Regulated Language Processing (SRLP) model (Stine-Morrow, Miller et al., 2006) is presented in Figure 1. Building on contemporary discourse theory, the SRLP model assumes that language understanding and memory arise from the creation of a multifaceted representation of the text’s lexical expression (surface form), its semantic content (ideas), and a perceptually rich representation of the situation suggested by the ideas. There are a number of component processes at each of these three levels (which we can label, word, textbase, and situation model representations) that are needed to construct this representation. Analysis of how process-relevant text characteristics affect reading times can provide indices of how responsive an individual reader is to these text-processing demands. For example, when controlling for surface features (e.g., word length and frequency), we find that readers allocate extra time to words that introduce new concepts, in order to instantiate their contextualized meanings, and that they allocate extra time at the ends of clauses and sentences, in order to integrate these meanings into ideas (for other examples, see Fig. 1). Individual differences in “allocation policy,” operationalized as the relative change in unit reading times as a function of variation in text characteristics (regression coefficients), provide a way to characterize the quality and extent of attentional engagement in reading.

A given individual’s allocation policy shows some consistency across time and across type of text, suggesting that patterns of resource allocation may reflect a “habit of mind” (Stine-Morrow, Miller, Gagne, & Hertzog, in press). Importantly, certain aspects of resource allocation--notably, conceptual integration and effort allocated to elaborate the discourse situation--predict memory and comprehension performance (Stine-Morrow, Gagne, Morrow, & DeWall, 2004; Stine-Morrow et al., in press). Thus, there is good evidence that how a reader cognitively engages a text determines what he or she will learn.

Age-related declines in language performance may be viewed, then, as arising partly from an allocation policy that does not effectively accommodate age-graded change in processing capacity. Successful older readers often show a variety of adjustments in allocation, such as exaggerated attention to conceptual integration, more frequent chunking of language input (i.e., pausing more frequently for conceptual integration), and greater allocation of attention to situational features and discourse structures (Stine-Morrow, Morrow, & Leno, 2002; Stine-Morrow et al., 2004; Stine-Morrow et al., in press). The upshot of these findings is that (implicit or explicit) choices about attentional engagement predict performance. What is less clear is what drives the recruitment of resources for effective attentional engagement.

A recent study in our lab suggests that exertion of effort may be related to both existing capacity and beliefs that effort will lead to good performance. In this study (Stine-Morrow, Shake, Miles, & Noh, 2006), adults read brief texts with the instruction either to read for accuracy or to read for efficiency. Even though the existence of age-related differences in processing capacity would suggest that older adults would need to allocate disproportionate reading time when accurate recall was required, the actual reading time patterns showed the reverse: It was younger readers whose time allocation was especially responsive to the accuracy goal (see Fig. 2). Interestingly, age differences in responsiveness to the accuracy goal (measured as the proportional difference in time allocation between the efficiency and accuracy conditions) could be accounted for in part by both working-memory capacity and memory self-efficacy (based on a paper-and-pencil assessment of beliefs that one can affect outcomes in memory performance). So, regardless of whether or not older adults were actually able to engage the task demands, age differences in self-efficacy contributed to their reluctance to do so. Unpublished data from our lab show that older readers can be induced to engage in more thorough semantic analysis with specific instruction in conceptual integration.

At the same time, it is undeniable that core cognitive abilities such as working-memory capacity and speed of processing powerfully contribute to cognitive vitality and performance in complex activities such as problem solving and language understanding and, in later adulthood, to the capability to perform simple instrumental activities of daily living (e.g., preparing a meal, paying bills; Willis et al., 2006). However, in spite of the frequent admonition to “use it or lose it,” there is some controversy over whether maintaining a lifestyle rich in intellectual activity actually forestalls cognitive decline (Salthouse, 2006).

Some evidence suggests that individuals embedded in contexts providing regular opportunities for intellectual challenge (e.g., demanding work environments that require frequent encounters with ill-defined problems; intellectually stimulating leisure pursuits) fare better along a number of dimensions. They are likely to live longer, be more resistant to psychological manifestations of age-related pathological processes (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, AD), and score better on measures of cognitive performance. While such findings are consistent with the idea that activity engagement has broad-based effects on vitality, they are fundamentally ambiguous, of course, because it may simply be that individuals with higher levels of inherent vitality are more likely to seek out such experiences and to be able to maintain their participation once they find them. In other words, it is plausible that occupational engagement, leisure activities, and vulnerability to dementia (and their trajectories over time) are all just biobehavioral markers for a genetically driven aging process that (as in most biobehavioral processes) is normally distributed in the population, so that these indicators of vitality simply correlate because they derive from a common cause.

Other evidence for plasticity of core abilities derives from training studies. Probably the most influential exemplar of such research is the ACTIVE trial (Willis et al., 2006), a randomized clinical trial in which older adults were provided 10 sessions of training in certain cognitive abilities (memory, speed, or reasoning). Relative to a no-contact control group, groups who received training showed effects of training, with retention demonstrated up to 5 years. Training effects were highly specific; for example, effects of speed-of-processing training were specific to speeded tasks and did not transfer to memory or reasoning. While there has been no demonstrated transfer to everyday analogues of these abilities, those who have received reasoning training report more ease with instrumental activities of daily living.

In contrast to studies investigating the correlational effects of engaged lifestyle and education, then, experimental training studies (along with studies of expert performance) suggest that the effects of experience are highly specific to the abilities trained. This is a puzzle. One plausible explanation is that an engaged lifestyle might simply exercise a wider array of abilities, but how that broad-based exercise of components might arise is largely unexplored. While engagement has been operationalized in different ways across studies, fundamentally it entails relatively high levels of challenging activity situated in real-life contexts that require self-direction. Central to this idea is that the novelty of the context and the ill-defined and nonroutine nature of the challenges encountered require the individual to make choices with respect to selecting, prioritizing, and structuring problems, as well as organizing resources to develop solutions.

As such, engagement is more akin to “education,” which is foundational preparation for thinking and acting in unpredictable contexts, than it is to “training,” which is preparation to perform a well-defined set of skills. It may be that the very things that are the hardest to control in empirical investigation--for example, choice in approaching novelty and ill-defined problems by exercising skills on an “as needed” basis--are the sources of the broad-based effects of activity engagement. Our research strategy has been to create a multimodal intervention that we believe exemplifies an engaged lifestyle and to experimentally test the effects of such engagement on a wide array of cognitive abilities and predispositions. The Senior Odyssey program (Stine-Morrow, Parisi, Morrow, Greene, & Park, 2007), characterized in Box 1, scaffolds onto existing social structures through linkages with an existing program, Odyssey of the Mind. Program development and evaluation of its effects are ongoing.

It may be that the very things that are the hardest to control in empirical investigation--for example, choice in approaching novelty and ill-defined problems by exercising skills on an “as needed” basis--are the sources of the broad-based effects of activity engagement. Our research strategy has been to create a multimodal intervention that we believe exemplifies an engaged lifestyle and to experimentally test the effects of such engagement on a wide array of cognitive abilities and predispositions. The Senior Odyssey program (Stine-Morrow, Parisi, Morrow, Greene, & Park, 2007), characterized in Box 1, scaffolds onto existing social structures through linkages with an existing program, Odyssey of the Mind. Program development and evaluation of its effects are ongoing.

결론

So what is the path to cognitive vitality through the life span? The essence of aging is the dynamic interplay between gains and losses. Losses come “for free”; gains are hard won. The literature on resource allocation suggests that there is considerable variability in the extent to which individuals engage the demands of an everyday cognitive task like reading. At the same time, it appears clear from studies of cognitive plasticity that behavioral effects of experience are extremely narrow, so that the effects of activity engagement on broad-based cognitive vitality likely arise because an engaged lifestyle engenders attentional engagement to develop and maintain skills needed for that lifestyle. Intellectually rich contexts that invite self-regulatory efficacy (an education/engagement model as opposed to a training model) may stimulate vitality by providing opportunities to exercise attentional engagement to skill development in ways that are sufficiently meaningful and emotionally satisfying to be self-perpetuating. Key issues that require empirical examination include conditions (e.g., novelty) that support the development and maintenance of attentional and activity engagement, the way in which these two forms of engagement interact, and psychosocial mechanisms (e.g., self-efficacy, control, mindfulness) through which exposure to novelty and nonroutine challenge might engender engagement. Such evidence will test the Dumbledore hypothesis that cognitive vitality depends on both how we engage the task demands of the activities we select and which abilities we sculpt through such activities. As we age, it may well be that it is our choices that show what we truly are.
