Evidence: ABS-001

Read the source passage

Cognitive aging is often characterized as a process in which two competing forces determine individual development: a genetically driven senescence process that engenders declines in mental mechanics; and an accumulation of life experience that augments cultural, pragmatic, and knowledge-based competence. The considerable variability in the level and rate of change in complex intellectual activities (e.g., language understanding) is often accounted for in terms of individual differences in abilities associated with these forces. I argue that choice in how effort is allocated may be an essential determinant of cognitive change over the life span--both directly, in the form of attentional engagement, and indirectly, as it sculpts neural substrates that give rise to component abilities.

Evidence: OPENING-001

Read the source passage

With life expectancies extended because of medical and other cultural advances, many individuals spend a quarter to a third of their lives in the period characterized as “old age,” a large proportion of which has great potential for vitality in health and mental activity. However, opportunities for intellectual activity often become restricted later in the life span (Riley & Riley, 2000), as do societal expectations for continued cognitive vitality (Hess, Auman, Colcombe, & Rahhal, 2003). In fact, cognitive aging is often characterized as a multifaceted phenomenon, encompassing both growth and decline (Baltes, 1997): While fluid abilities--“mental mechanics” that depend on speeded encoding and transformation of novel information--normatively show declines as a consequence of a genetically driven senescence process, crystallized abilities that depend on knowledge and acquisition of culture can remain stable or increase well into late life. Declines in fluid abilities are a source of concern to researchers and are often deeply disturbing to aging individuals because they are so readily observable: Difficulties with memory, attentional control, and language understanding can hinder a person from participating in everyday life.

The question raised in this article is whether individuals have some degree of control over these developmental trajectories. In some ways, as we age, we are put in much the same situation as the fictional wizard Harry Potter when he dons the “sorting hat” that selects the house to which he will be assigned at the wizarding school Hogwarts (Rowling, 1997). It is a hard decision for the sorting hat because Harry has certain characteristics of the noble wizards of the house of Gryffindor and the evil wizards of the house of Slytherin (who are also clever and decisive in difficult situations). Hearing the sorting hat’s murmurings, Harry asks not to be put into Slytherin, but into Gryffindor. The hat tries to convince him otherwise, but Harry insists, and the hat finally agrees. Subsequently, as Harry faces terrible ordeals requiring great courage, this interaction troubles him as he wonders whether he is “truly Gryffindor” or “truly Slytherin.”

Similarly, as we grow older, we struggle with the “sorting hat” of cultural expectations about aging. When faced with intellectual challenges requiring great courage (e.g., keeping track of names at a party, learning new technology, or deciphering retirement plans), we wonder whether we are truly up to the task. We make (implicit and explicit) choices to seek out and engage cognitively challenging situations in meaningful and mindful ways or else to withdraw from activities--either because we buy into cultural stereotypes of diminished cognitive capacity or because opportunities for meaningful intellectual challenge are less available, or both. We often make this choice in cultural contexts in which intellectual capacity is viewed as a fixed entity that cannot be substantially altered through effort (Molden & Dweck, 2006) and in which older adults very typically do not expect to be able to control cognitive outcomes (Hess et al., 2003). To the extent that we do withdraw from intellectual activity, it is possible that performance deteriorates because task-relevant processes are not implemented, as well as because disuse may exaggerate senescence-related declines in cognitive function (which underpins performance).

Even after Harry Potter has performed remarkable feats, he is plagued by doubts, which he discusses with headmaster Dumbledore. Dumbledore reminds Harry of all that he has accomplished and assures him that, “It is our choices . . . that show what we truly are, far more than our abilities” (Rowling, 1999, p. 333). The current article considers the viability of the Dumbledore hypothesis of cognitive aging--the theory that it is our pattern of choice to engage intellectual challenge that contributes to cognitive vitality, far more than the senescence process.

Evidence: ATTENTION-001

Read the source passage

Choosing to engage in intellectual activity may be an important determinant of cognitive optimization in two very different senses. First, assuming that cognitive performance ultimately arises from the execution of cognitive operations that underpin that performance, outcomes will be enhanced if computations are conducted effectively (e.g., allocating effort to organizational and elaborative processing for good memory); we can call this implementation of task-specific processes attentional engagement. In other words, effective allocation of attentional resources is important in any cognitive task just to get the job done. The example of language processing will be used to illustrate this principle (Stine-Morrow, Miller, & Hertzog, 2006). Second, habitually selecting environmental contexts that are intellectually stimulating--or activity engagement--may potentially expand capacity (Kramer, Bherer, Colcombe, Dong, & Greenough, 2004). Thus, I propose that habitual self-regulation of one’s cognitive processing and one’s environmental context may contribute to intellectual vitality in later adulthood, both by augmenting performance and by sculpting the cognitive system that engenders future performance.

In spite of the fact that verbal ability is a crystallized ability that often shows resilience into old age, memory for spoken and written language often reveals declines with age (Johnson, 2003). In our lab, we have investigated the extent to which these deficits may be rooted in nonoptimal attentional engagement. A visual representation of our Self-Regulated Language Processing (SRLP) model (Stine-Morrow, Miller et al., 2006) is presented in Figure 1. Building on contemporary discourse theory, the SRLP model assumes that language understanding and memory arise from the creation of a multifaceted representation of the text’s lexical expression (surface form), its semantic content (ideas), and a perceptually rich representation of the situation suggested by the ideas. There are a number of component processes at each of these three levels (which we can label, word, textbase, and situation model representations) that are needed to construct this representation. Analysis of how process-relevant text characteristics affect reading times can provide indices of how responsive an individual reader is to these text-processing demands. For example, when controlling for surface features (e.g., word length and frequency), we find that readers allocate extra time to words that introduce new concepts, in order to instantiate their contextualized meanings, and that they allocate extra time at the ends of clauses and sentences, in order to integrate these meanings into ideas (for other examples, see Fig. 1). Individual differences in “allocation policy,” operationalized as the relative change in unit reading times as a function of variation in text characteristics (regression coefficients), provide a way to characterize the quality and extent of attentional engagement in reading.

Discussion question 04

Explain the negative feedback loops in the SRLP model.SRLP 모형의 부적 피드백 순환을 설명해 보세요.

30-second answer

At the surface-form, textbase, and situation-model levels, readers compare their perceived understanding with a reference value. If understanding is not good enough, the relevant processor requests more resources until perceived comprehension meets the criterion. Negative feedback means regulation that reduces a discrepancy, rather than a harmful effect. The reference values are influenced by readers' cognitive and socioemotional goals.독자는 표면 형태, 텍스트 기반, 상황모형의 각 수준에서 자신이 지각한 이해 정도를 기준값과 비교합니다. 이해가 충분하지 않으면 해당 처리기는 지각된 이해가 기준에 도달할 때까지 더 많은 자원을 요청합니다. 부적 피드백은 해로운 효과가 아니라 불일치를 줄이는 조절을 뜻합니다. 기준값은 독자의 인지적 목표와 사회정서적 목표의 영향을 받습니다.

Read the source passage

A given individual’s allocation policy shows some consistency across time and across type of text, suggesting that patterns of resource allocation may reflect a “habit of mind” (Stine-Morrow, Miller, Gagne, & Hertzog, in press). Importantly, certain aspects of resource allocation--notably, conceptual integration and effort allocated to elaborate the discourse situation--predict memory and comprehension performance (Stine-Morrow, Gagne, Morrow, & DeWall, 2004; Stine-Morrow et al., in press). Thus, there is good evidence that how a reader cognitively engages a text determines what he or she will learn.

Age-related declines in language performance may be viewed, then, as arising partly from an allocation policy that does not effectively accommodate age-graded change in processing capacity. Successful older readers often show a variety of adjustments in allocation, such as exaggerated attention to conceptual integration, more frequent chunking of language input (i.e., pausing more frequently for conceptual integration), and greater allocation of attention to situational features and discourse structures (Stine-Morrow, Morrow, & Leno, 2002; Stine-Morrow et al., 2004; Stine-Morrow et al., in press). The upshot of these findings is that (implicit or explicit) choices about attentional engagement predict performance. What is less clear is what drives the recruitment of resources for effective attentional engagement.

A recent study in our lab suggests that exertion of effort may be related to both existing capacity and beliefs that effort will lead to good performance. In this study (Stine-Morrow, Shake, Miles, & Noh, 2006), adults read brief texts with the instruction either to read for accuracy or to read for efficiency. Even though the existence of age-related differences in processing capacity would suggest that older adults would need to allocate disproportionate reading time when accurate recall was required, the actual reading time patterns showed the reverse: It was younger readers whose time allocation was especially responsive to the accuracy goal (see Fig. 2). Interestingly, age differences in responsiveness to the accuracy goal (measured as the proportional difference in time allocation between the efficiency and accuracy conditions) could be accounted for in part by both working-memory capacity and memory self-efficacy (based on a paper-and-pencil assessment of beliefs that one can affect outcomes in memory performance). So, regardless of whether or not older adults were actually able to engage the task demands, age differences in self-efficacy contributed to their reluctance to do so. Unpublished data from our lab show that older readers can be induced to engage in more thorough semantic analysis with specific instruction in conceptual integration.

At the same time, it appears clear from studies of cognitive plasticity that behavioral effects of experience are extremely narrow, so that the effects of activity engagement on broad-based cognitive vitality likely arise because an engaged lifestyle engenders attentional engagement to develop and maintain skills needed for that lifestyle. Intellectually rich contexts that invite self-regulatory efficacy (an education/engagement model as opposed to a training model) may stimulate vitality by providing opportunities to exercise attentional engagement to skill development in ways that are sufficiently meaningful and emotionally satisfying to be self-perpetuating. Key issues that require empirical examination include conditions (e.g., novelty) that support the development and maintenance of attentional and activity engagement, the way in which these two forms of engagement interact, and psychosocial mechanisms (e.g., self-efficacy, control, mindfulness) through which exposure to novelty and nonroutine challenge might engender engagement. Such evidence will test the Dumbledore hypothesis that cognitive vitality depends on both how we engage the task demands of the activities we select and which abilities we sculpt through such activities. As we age, it may well be that it is our choices that show what we truly are.
