Public talk and private aging공적 담론과 사적인 노화

Why did the gap between public and private talk stand out?공적 담론과 사적 경험의 간극이 왜 눈에 들어왔나요?

첫 답변

I found it interesting that aging researchers can publicly celebrate active aging while privately discussing bodily limits and uncertainty. The gap seemed more consequential than a difference in wording: it can make older people into a separate 'them.' I read personal experience as a starting point for asking what the public account leaves difficult to express, rather than as an answer everyone must share.노화를 연구하는 사람도 공식적인 자리에서는 활동적인 노년을 강조하면서, 사적으로는 몸의 한계와 불확실성을 이야기한다는 점이 흥미로웠습니다. 이 간극이 단순한 표현의 차이가 아니라, 노인을 자신과 다른 ‘그들’로 만드는 문제라는 점이 새로웠습니다. 저는 연구자의 개인적 경험을 모두에게 적용할 정답으로 삼기보다, 기존의 공적인 노화 담론이 무엇을 드러내기 어렵게 하는지 묻는 출발점으로 읽었습니다.

근거: PERSPECTIVE-001

원문 근거 읽기

Today’s gerontologists often carry on two distinct discourses about aging: one public and scientific; the other private and personal. These discussions are typically at odds. The public voice lauds active aging; the private one secretly confesses limitations and precariousness. At professional meetings, we have also observed pervasive “othering,” as even the most senior gerontologists refer to old people as “they,” not “we.” Reflections about personal aging constitute, literally, another story. This pattern reproduces agism and stigma. Separating private and public discourses may seriously limit insights, both about processes of aging and the societies in which we live.

Einstein is quoted as saying “any scientific problem worth its salt starts with a personal problem.” The potential scientific costs of the dual aging discourses have received little attention. One exception is Hendricks (2008, p. 113):

Those of us who study aging have the unique opportunity to live their subject matter. As social gerontologists, we have been quick to assert that broad social currents carry persons to the doorstep of old age and beyond, but we have been far less reflective about our own journey.

Hendricks cites Schütz, who wrote extensively on the concept of meaning, which is also central in our framework. We see personal meaning as interpersonal. A sense of self is created and maintained through social mirrors, in webs of interdependent lives that span family generations and historical time. As societies change, so do socially constructed meanings. Consequently, the older individuals get, the more likely it is that they have core meanings that are not shared by many people around them, and they have few witnesses who know where they have been. To some extent, then, they become strangers to other people and even to themselves.

Novelists and social scientists have commented on the strangeness of old age. Scott-Maxwell (1968, p. 5) noted “Being old, I am out of step … Feeling at variance with the times must be the essence of age.” de Beauvoir (1970, p. 283) said “old age is particularly difficult to assume because we have always regarded it as something alien, a foreign species.” M. Mead (1970) observed that in rapidly changing societies, the old become “immigrants in time.” Later, Hendricks (1980) described old age as “another country.”

In exploring the territory of old age, we draw from the “Chicago School” of sociology, especially the symbolic interaction perspective. It offers insights into how humans create and maintain a sense of self and continuity across social borders—including phases of life, cultures, and social structures. Both of us have had life paths that crossed numerous such borders. We share experiences of being strangers.

Two classical pieces help us understand our border crossings: Simmel’s (1908) and Schütz’s (1944) essays on The Stranger. Both authors crossed borders between nations, disciplines, and professions. For Simmel, the stranger experiences a “unity of nearness and remoteness,” as well as “freedom” gained from a combination of “indifference and involvement.” Schütz pointed to the stranger’s ability to challenge a “tested system of recipes.” A stranger will ask “How?” and “Why?” about phenomena natives take as given. Our personal stories will illustrate how we have been in the position of the stranger before old age. These experiences make us challenge some common “recipes” in contemporary gerontology.

The authors describe older researchers discussing old people as a group apart from themselves, while keeping their own limitations private. Their concern is that this separation reproduces stigma and prevents personal aging from informing scholarship. What can personal accounts establish?개인적 이야기로 무엇을 알 수 있나요?

The essay combines the authors' experiences with theoretical concepts and insights from other writers. This can reveal meanings and suggest questions about aging, but it is neither a representative survey nor an experiment estimating a causal effect. I would explain what an account helps us notice without claiming that it shows how common that experience is.이 글은 저자들의 경험을 이론적 개념, 다른 필자들의 통찰과 함께 다룹니다. 이를 통해 노화의 의미를 드러내고 질문을 제기할 수 있지만, 대표성 있는 조사나 인과효과를 추정하는 실험은 아닙니다. 저는 그 이야기가 무엇을 보게 하는지 설명하되, 그 경험이 얼마나 흔한지까지 알려 준다고 하지는 않겠습니다.

We start with the observation that aging gerontologists often engage in two distinct discourses on aging—one public and one private. This separation entails “othering,” which reproduces agism and stigma. Based on personal experience, insight from colleagues and writers, and concepts from symbolic interaction perspectives, we argue that becoming old to some degree involves becoming a stranger. Before reaching old age, both of us have been in the position of strangers due to social experiences that left us “off the line” or “on the margins.” Examples are crossing social borders related to nations, class structures, gender, race, health status, and generations. Our stories illustrate how aging is more than personal. It is interpersonal—shaped by social history, policies, interdependence in relationships, and the precariousness of old age. Such phenomena often show sharp contrasts in the interpersonal worlds and social experiences of women and men. Reflecting on our own journeys as life course migrants leaves us acutely aware of both the social problems and potential promises of aging.

Social mirrors and co-biographers사회적 거울과 삶의 공동 기록자

What changed how you understood the loss of old relationships?오래된 관계의 상실을 새롭게 보게 한 점은 무엇인가요?

첫 답변

What felt new was that losing a longstanding relationship can mean more than losing company or practical help. A co-biographer remembers the life we have shared and helps sustain its continuity. Losing that person can also make it harder to connect who I am now with who I was. I became interested in what memories and meanings a relationship holds, beyond simply counting contacts.오래된 관계를 잃는 일이 도움이나 말벗을 잃는 것에서 끝나지 않는다는 점이 가장 새로웠습니다. 저자들이 말하는 ‘삶의 공동 기록자’는 내가 지나온 시간을 함께 기억하며 생애 이야기의 연속성을 확인하도록 돕는 사람입니다. 그래서 관계의 상실은 지금의 내가 과거의 나와 어떻게 이어지는지 설명하는 일도 어렵게 할 수 있습니다. 저는 관계의 수뿐 아니라 어떤 기억과 의미를 함께 지닌 관계인지에 관심을 갖게 됐습니다.

근거: CHICAGO-001

원문 근거 읽기

Both Simmel and Schütz had connections to the Chicago School and, especially in the case of Schütz, to symbolic interaction. He built from key works such as Mead’s (1934) discussion of the “social self,” and Cooley’s (1902) writing on the “looking glass self.” Both were central reading for us as students. Hagestad also read an account by a man who had spent 1 year in total isolation at 74° North. In front of the mirror, he would recite events from his past life. He commented, “You see, I had this feeling of not knowing who I was!”

Mead described how an individual develops a self that is shaped by others who represent norms and scripts in key primary groups and the wider society. There are important issues that Mead did not consider, such as the power of social scripts in late life, and impact of a changing body on the self.

Other scholars within the Chicago tradition continue to influence us, especially Goffman’s (1959) Presentation of self in everyday life. Goffman argues that humans seek desirable reflections on themselves in social encounters. He also discussed “spoiled identity” in his volume on stigma (Goffman, 1963). Stigma is strongly related to stereotypes and separates “us” from “them.” Below, we describe this phenomenon as “othering” (Said, 1979).

To us, a central aspect of understanding aging as interpersonal is that the older we are, the more likely it is that we have experienced the pain of losing longstanding relationships with others who constitute the “library of our lives.” Some authors use the terms “consociates” (Plath, 1980) or “co-biographers” for such others (Bertaux, 1981). Kahn and Antonucci (1980) discuss convoys of support. Recently, G. O. Hagestad had the painful realization that her 92-year-old aunt is the sole person who has known her since she was born. Those of us who are life course migrants have difficulties finding co-biographers when it is time to compose a life history. As Erikson (1986) claimed, the key developmental task in old age is to find integrity—threads of continuity in one’s life history. Butler (1963) called it life review. Experienced clinicians report that they are increasingly asked to help individuals compose a “good ending.” The two of us have only started writing our histories, but work on this essay has accelerated the process!

Are social mirrors and co-biographers the same thing?사회적 거울과 공동 기록자는 같은 뜻인가요?

They overlap, but emphasize different things. Social mirrors concern how a sense of self takes shape through others and their responses; co-biographers are people who know and share parts of our life history. A brief encounter can reflect an image of me without the other person knowing my past, while a longstanding relationship can also help connect that past to the present.겹치지만 강조하는 점이 다릅니다. 사회적 거울은 타인과 그 반응을 통해 자기인식이 형성되는 측면을, 공동 기록자는 내 생애의 일부를 알고 함께한 사람을 가리킵니다. 잠깐 만난 사람도 내 과거를 몰라도 나에 관한 이미지를 비출 수 있고, 오래된 관계는 그 과거를 현재와 연결하는 일까지 도울 수 있습니다.

Since my illness, I respond like my mother when plans are being made: “if I live.” Often, the response is “don’t say that!” Reactions illustrate Blythe’s (1979) statement: “we place dying in what we take to be its logical position, which is at the close of a long life—when our ancestors accepted the futility of placing it in any position at all” (p. 12). Fellow gerontologist Hendricks, also a cancer survivor in his 70s, reports similar reactions. Both of us feel out of step with cohort peers and sense a denial of finitude. I wonder if our illness experiences make it easier for Joe and me to be old, because we have faced premature precariousness. We have both also “gone public” with personal accounts of cancer, trying to reduce stigma. My body carries scars and damage left by a bombardment of broad-spectrum cancer treatment which, in my 70s, join forces with “normal aging,” such as loss of bone mass and muscle strength. Because of my illness two decades ago, I may be more ready than many peers to accept old age, also when it brings pain and reduced energy. Brody (2010) writes humorously about her own aging: “I don’t remember becoming old.” Personally, I do remember the day that I became old—or incorporated old age in my sense of self. I was in my 60s, going through security at an airport. An officer called out, “The shoes on the belt belong to the old lady!” Looking around, I saw no old woman. The looking glass was there, and the self got the message! Since then, the list of things I can no longer do has gotten longer. I doubt that I will ever ski again, at least down hills, and I have climbed my last mountain. But I have a daily regiment of exercise and (short!) walks in the woods; I still love gardening (in a garden that has shrunk). I am retired, but people call me “still active.” We academics are lucky in that we can to some degree “keep on working” as long as we can think. I am fortunate to still be invited to give occasional keynotes and informal presentations in several countries, and I still write!

Today’s gerontologists often carry on two distinct discourses about aging: one public and scientific; the other private and personal. These discussions are typically at odds. The public voice lauds active aging; the private one secretly confesses limitations and precariousness. At professional meetings, we have also observed pervasive “othering,” as even the most senior gerontologists refer to old people as “they,” not “we.” Reflections about personal aging constitute, literally, another story. This pattern reproduces agism and stigma. Separating private and public discourses may seriously limit insights, both about processes of aging and the societies in which we live.

Einstein is quoted as saying “any scientific problem worth its salt starts with a personal problem.” The potential scientific costs of the dual aging discourses have received little attention. One exception is Hendricks (2008, p. 113):

Those of us who study aging have the unique opportunity to live their subject matter. As social gerontologists, we have been quick to assert that broad social currents carry persons to the doorstep of old age and beyond, but we have been far less reflective about our own journey.

Hendricks cites Schütz, who wrote extensively on the concept of meaning, which is also central in our framework. We see personal meaning as interpersonal. A sense of self is created and maintained through social mirrors, in webs of interdependent lives that span family generations and historical time. As societies change, so do socially constructed meanings. Consequently, the older individuals get, the more likely it is that they have core meanings that are not shared by many people around them, and they have few witnesses who know where they have been. Novelists and social scientists have commented on the strangeness of old age. Scott-Maxwell (1968, p. 5) noted “Being old, I am out of step … Feeling at variance with the times must be the essence of age.” de Beauvoir (1970, p. 283) said “old age is particularly difficult to assume because we have always regarded it as something alien, a foreign species.” M. In exploring the territory of old age, we draw from the “Chicago School” of sociology, especially the symbolic interaction perspective. It offers insights into how humans create and maintain a sense of self and continuity across social borders—including phases of life, cultures, and social structures. QUESTION 04 · 연결된 세대를 통해 전해지는 역사

Why should a life-course explanation include both early conditions and later changes?생애과정 설명에서 초기 조건과 이후의 변화를 모두 살펴봐야 하는 이유는 무엇인가요?

1분 답변

Vaupel reviews evidence that early conditions can matter for later health, but he does not make childhood destiny. He also emphasizes adult smoking, medical progress, and economic changes later in life; mortality convergence after German reunification illustrates the relevance of changing conditions. Hagestad and Settersten widen the historical lens further. Their accounts show that parents' and grandparents' experiences can shape meanings, expectations, and relationships across generations, beyond a person's own cohort location. I would therefore ask when an exposure occurred, what changed afterward, and whose history enters the person's life. This connects demographic and interpersonal accounts without assuming that a remembered family experience directly caused a particular disease or mortality outcome.Vaupel은 초기 조건이 이후 건강에 중요할 수 있다는 근거를 검토하지만, 아동기를 운명으로 보지는 않습니다. 성인기의 흡연, 의학적 진전, 생애 후반의 경제적 변화도 강조하며, 독일 통일 이후 사망률이 수렴한 사례는 조건 변화의 중요성을 보여 줍니다. QUESTION 05 · 젠더·생존·관계의 교차

Why might longer survival coexist with more illness and different relational burdens for women?여성에게 더 긴 생존이 더 많은 질병 및 다른 관계적 부담과 함께 나타날 수 있는 이유는 무엇인가요?

1분 답변

Vaupel describes a health–survival paradox: older women often survive longer despite poorer health on several measures, while men can appear healthier yet die earlier. He stresses interacting social and biological factors rather than one settled explanation. Hagestad and Settersten consider how gendered histories of caregiving and relationship investment shape later-life interdependence. Longer lives and enduring ties can mean more available support and shared memories, but also exposure to loss and obligations. Reading them together, I would distinguish a population pattern of survival and morbidity from an account of how relationships distribute its consequences. Neither pattern means that all women provide care, all men lack support, or any individual follows the average.Vaupel은 여러 지표에서 고령 여성이 건강 상태가 더 나쁜데도 더 오래 생존하고, 남성은 더 건강해 보이면서 더 일찍 사망할 수 있는 건강–생존 역설을 설명합니다. 하나의 확정된 설명 대신 사회적·생물학적 요인의 상호작용을 강조합니다. Hagestad와 Settersten은 돌봄과 관계에 대한 투자가 젠더에 따라 달랐던 역사가 노년의 상호의존을 어떻게 형성하는지 살펴봅니다. 긴 삶과 오래 지속된 유대는 더 많은 지원과 공유된 기억을 뜻할 수 있지만, 상실과 의무에 노출되는 경험도 동반할 수 있습니다. 두 글을 함께 읽으며 저는 생존·질병의 인구 패턴과 관계가 그 결과를 어떻게 분배하는지에 관한 설명을 구분하겠습니다. 어느 패턴도 모든 여성이 돌봄을 제공하거나 모든 남성이 지원이 부족하며, 모든 개인이 평균을 따른다는 뜻은 아닙니다.

한 문장으로 줄이면

QUESTION 06 · 일과 돌봄의 생애 재배치

How could a longer-life society reorganize work without simply requiring everyone to work longer?장수사회는 모두에게 더 오래 일하라고 요구하는 것 이상으로 노동을 어떻게 재구성할 수 있을까요?

1분 답변

Vaupel proposes spreading work across more years while reducing weekly hours, leaving more room for education, childrearing, and leisure throughout life. Hagestad and Settersten show why the relational consequences deserve attention: care responsibilities connect family members, and leave or pension rules can support one role while penalizing another. Longer survival creates a reason to reconsider timetables, but demographic averages alone cannot tell us which individuals can fairly bear additional work.Vaupel은 더 많은 연수에 걸쳐 일하되 주당 노동시간을 줄여, 생애 전반에 교육·자녀 양육·여가를 위한 여지를 늘리는 방안을 제안합니다. 독일의 예시는 2005년 기준선과 가상의 2025년 구성을 비교한 것이며, 그런 개혁이 실제로 이루어졌다는 보고는 아닙니다.
