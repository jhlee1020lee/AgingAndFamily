01

What process places older people in a separate category of them rather than including them in us?

Accepted answers: othering

Explanation: The separation of public and private aging discourses can reproduce this distancing process, along with ageism and stigma.

Evidence: PERSPECTIVE-001 · Public and private discourses

Read the source passage

Today’s gerontologists often carry on two distinct discourses about aging: one public and scientific; the other private and personal. These discussions are typically at odds. The public voice lauds active aging; the private one secretly confesses limitations and precariousness. At professional meetings, we have also observed pervasive “othering,” as even the most senior gerontologists refer to old people as “they,” not “we.” Reflections about personal aging constitute, literally, another story. This pattern reproduces agism and stigma. Separating private and public discourses may seriously limit insights, both about processes of aging and the societies in which we live.

Einstein is quoted as saying “any scientific problem worth its salt starts with a personal problem.” The potential scientific costs of the dual aging discourses have received little attention. One exception is Hendricks (2008, p. 113):

Those of us who study aging have the unique opportunity to live their subject matter. As social gerontologists, we have been quick to assert that broad social currents carry persons to the doorstep of old age and beyond, but we have been far less reflective about our own journey.

Hendricks cites Schütz, who wrote extensively on the concept of meaning, which is also central in our framework. We see personal meaning as interpersonal. A sense of self is created and maintained through social mirrors, in webs of interdependent lives that span family generations and historical time. As societies change, so do socially constructed meanings. Consequently, the older individuals get, the more likely it is that they have core meanings that are not shared by many people around them, and they have few witnesses who know where they have been. To some extent, then, they become strangers to other people and even to themselves.

Novelists and social scientists have commented on the strangeness of old age. Scott-Maxwell (1968, p. 5) noted “Being old, I am out of step … Feeling at variance with the times must be the essence of age.” de Beauvoir (1970, p. 283) said “old age is particularly difficult to assume because we have always regarded it as something alien, a foreign species.” M. Mead (1970) observed that in rapidly changing societies, the old become “immigrants in time.” Later, Hendricks (1980) described old age as “another country.”

In exploring the territory of old age, we draw from the “Chicago School” of sociology, especially the symbolic interaction perspective. It offers insights into how humans create and maintain a sense of self and continuity across social borders—including phases of life, cultures, and social structures. Both of us have had life paths that crossed numerous such borders. We share experiences of being strangers.

Two classical pieces help us understand our border crossings: Simmel’s (1908) and Schütz’s (1944) essays on The Stranger. Both authors crossed borders between nations, disciplines, and professions. For Simmel, the stranger experiences a “unity of nearness and remoteness,” as well as “freedom” gained from a combination of “indifference and involvement.” Schütz pointed to the stranger’s ability to challenge a “tested system of recipes.” A stranger will ask “How?” and “Why?” about phenomena natives take as given. Our personal stories will illustrate how we have been in the position of the stranger before old age. These experiences make us challenge some common “recipes” in contemporary gerontology.

03

What Cooley concept describes the self through reflections encountered in other people's responses?

Accepted answers: looking-glass self / looking glass self / the looking-glass self / the looking glass self

Explanation: Social mirrors help explain the interpersonal construction of identity, including the meanings of an aging body.

Evidence: CHICAGO-001 · Symbolic interaction

Read the source passage

Both Simmel and Schütz had connections to the Chicago School and, especially in the case of Schütz, to symbolic interaction. He built from key works such as Mead’s (1934) discussion of the “social self,” and Cooley’s (1902) writing on the “looking glass self.” Both were central reading for us as students. Hagestad also read an account by a man who had spent 1 year in total isolation at 74° North. In front of the mirror, he would recite events from his past life. He commented, “You see, I had this feeling of not knowing who I was!”

Mead described how an individual develops a self that is shaped by others who represent norms and scripts in key primary groups and the wider society. There are important issues that Mead did not consider, such as the power of social scripts in late life, and impact of a changing body on the self.

Other scholars within the Chicago tradition continue to influence us, especially Goffman’s (1959) Presentation of self in everyday life. Goffman argues that humans seek desirable reflections on themselves in social encounters. He also discussed “spoiled identity” in his volume on stigma (Goffman, 1963). Stigma is strongly related to stereotypes and separates “us” from “them.” Below, we describe this phenomenon as “othering” (Said, 1979).

To us, a central aspect of understanding aging as interpersonal is that the older we are, the more likely it is that we have experienced the pain of losing longstanding relationships with others who constitute the “library of our lives.” Some authors use the terms “consociates” (Plath, 1980) or “co-biographers” for such others (Bertaux, 1981). Kahn and Antonucci (1980) discuss convoys of support. Recently, G. O. Hagestad had the painful realization that her 92-year-old aunt is the sole person who has known her since she was born. Those of us who are life course migrants have difficulties finding co-biographers when it is time to compose a life history. As Erikson (1986) claimed, the key developmental task in old age is to find integrity—threads of continuity in one’s life history. Butler (1963) called it life review. Experienced clinicians report that they are increasingly asked to help individuals compose a “good ending.” The two of us have only started writing our histories, but work on this essay has accelerated the process!

What term do the authors use for people whose pathways cross social boundaries and familiar life scripts?

Accepted answers: life course migrant / life course migrants / life-course migrant / life-course migrants

Explanation: Their autobiographies describe crossings of national, class, gender, family, and other social boundaries.

Evidence: MIGRANTS-001 · The authors' positions

Read the source passage

We now turn to brief autobiographical accounts of our life paths, which first intersected in the late 1980s through our roles as professor and PhD student at Northwestern University. We soon found commonality in experiences as strangers, having both been migrants, but in quite different ways. Since then, we have had more than 30 years of shared history, with Hagestad aging from her 40s to 70s and Settersten from his 20s to 50s.

In our collaboration (e.g., Settersten & Hagestad, 1996) we have written about “the normal, expectable life” (Neugarten, 1969). Such socially shared scripts of life include expectations about the timing and sequencing of role transitions. Our thinking about life pathways also builds on the life course perspective (e.g., Dannefer & Settersten, 2010; Hagestad & Dykstra, 2016). Here, we extend two central principles from this perspective: that aging is shaped by historical time and by interdependence with others. We also join an ongoing discourse on “precariousness” and aging (Grenier, Lloyd, & Phillipson, in press).

In 1966, I married a foreigner—a Hungarian student, who was part of an exchange program with Norway. We moved from Europe to the American Midwest, where I had been an exchange student in high school. While working on our PhDs, we had two daughters. The marriage ended in the 1970s, and I spent years as a single mom. Since then, my daughters, who reside in the United States, have become mothers, giving me the blessing of grandchildren. Both are travelers, having visited several continents and lived for periods of time in Japan and Norway. I am proud of their ability to communicate across borders.

After three decades in the United States, my home base is now the rural community where my father grew up. Because I live in my great-grandfather’s house, and have my grandmother’s name, many locals place me in my family tree. Yet, I am also “the professor” and, to some extent, seen as a stranger, an American. I live part of the year in the United States, where I am also considered a “foreigner,” a European. Both places, I experience Simmel’s unity of nearness and remoteness, as well as freedom.

Shortly after which birthday was GOH diagnosed with stage IV breast cancer?

Accepted answers: 50 / 50th / 50th birthday / fiftieth / fiftieth birthday

Explanation: The diagnosis shortly after this birthday brought treatment and a premature encounter with precariousness.

Evidence: GOH-COHORT-001 · GOH's illness

Read the source passage

Most of my childhood, we lived in a rural schoolhouse that bore marks left by occupying military troops. Our cohort location left permanent marks on our bodies and values. Nutrition was limited in years of rationing. Many members of my cohort have poor teeth and muscle-skeletal problems. We did not have inoculations against common childhood diseases, nor antibiotics. A close cousin died of polio. Given childhood experiences, I have difficulties throwing things out, be it leftover food or used clothing. Because family solidarity was crucial in hard times, kin ties are important to me. We have an annual family reunion at the family farm, where my brother now lives.

My cohort location also brought opportunities my parents did not have. As new policies increased access to universities, I became part of an educational vanguard and had many experiences of being “the first” or “the only.” I was the only PhD student in my cohort who had children. Starting a position at Penn State in the late 1970s, I was the only woman faculty member in my unit who was a mother. In early phases of my career, I was often the only woman in the room during national and international meetings. Such experiences taught me how being a statistical outlier makes it difficult to build a convoy of peer support—both in professional and family roles. However, I was fortunate to have two women mentors and role models: philosopher May Brodbeck in graduate school and colleague Bernice Neugarten in my first academic job. Early career years form a stark contrast to life after 70 years of age, which has brought a sense of being invisible, and being called “sweetie” and “honey” in the United States!

A sense of being a lone traveler was at its strongest when I was diagnosed with stage IV breast cancer in 1993, shortly after my 50th birthday. Before turning 51, I had surgery, chemotherapy and radiation. After treatments, I developed life-threatening infections. In those critical 2 years, the possibility of death was very real. The hardest part of facing death was linked to interdependence and scripts of normal, expectable lives. I felt that my two daughters were too young to become motherless, and I had an intense hope to see them become mothers and me a grandmother. My mother was extremely shaken, saying, “I can’t outlive one of my children!”

What phrase describes the shift of central and peripheral people in GOH's support network after illness?

Accepted answers: reorganized convoy / reorganized convoys / reorganised convoy / reorganised convoys / convoy reorganization / convoy reorganisation

Explanation: Some central people disappeared, while previously peripheral people, including her sister, became crucial sources of support.

Evidence: GOH-ILLNESS-001 · Support convoys

Read the source passage

The illness and its aftermath brought back issues raised in my philosophy courses, especially Feigl’s (1967) discussion of mind/body dualism. I kept thinking “if this is my body, where am I supposed to go?” Later, I realized that I had a preview of old age: experiencing premature precariousness. This was not only a personal crisis but also an interpersonal one. Consociates could not understand that I was unable to fully participate in everyday life. I wanted to say: I am not unreliable; my body is! American and Norwegian cultures both emphasize “attitude is key! Pull yourself together!” Many proponents of “active aging” start from such premises, which may entail problematic dichotomous thinking: active/passive, independent/dependent, and able/disabled. Once labeled ill, disabled or dependent on help, you often lose the opportunity to use the capacity—albeit limited—that you still have. In nursing homes, residents are often blocked from participating in activities they could master.

The illness made me fall out of time (Hagestad, 1996), creating dramatic insights into how adult roles are built on assumptions about a healthy body, predictable linear time, and expected durations. When I no longer conformed to normal timetable scripts, people were hesitant to include me in plans. Part of this reluctance was due to stigma. Cancer scares people, and many feel a need to “other” the patient. The role of patient overshadowed all others that were at the core of my sense of self: mother, sister, daughter, friend, and professor.

Like many cancer patients, I experienced reorganized convoys: central persons disappeared; peripheral individuals moved to the center. In this process, I became very close to my sister, who knows a great deal about being a patient. Growing up, we did not really know each other, since she is 8 years my junior. Before reaching adulthood, she was diagnosed with a rare autoimmune syndrome, which has created a number of near-death situations. On many of those occasions, we were on different sides of the Atlantic. During my illness, she became a crucial support person. My brother, who is a medical doctor, also provided lifesaving insight and help. When we recently celebrated his 70th birthday, we sisters said that without him, neither of us would have lived to experience the party. Supportive others can make an enormous difference in reducing discontinuity, even death. If a colleague had not nominated me to give the opening address for the UN International Year of Older Persons (Hagestad, 1998), an honor that provided visibility, I may not have been able to resume academic work.

Which 2015 US Supreme Court decision figures in RAS's account of marriage recognition?

Explanation: The essay links this historical decision to marriage recognition and spousal protections, while describing uncertainty surrounding its implementation at the time.

Evidence: RAS-LAW-001 · Legal recognition

Refugees of sorts, we have had to chase the law in order to secure basic protection—wandering in search of kinder policies. In 2006, we uprooted our family from Ohio in order to live in a state (Oregon) that would allow us to co-adopt our children. In 2014, we were allowed to marry in the state of Washington and be recognized as spouses in Oregon. Despite the fact that we are a long-lived couple, the clock to social security spousal benefits only started to tick in June 2015, with the landmark Supreme Court decision of Obergefell v. Hodges. Although this ruling should guarantee same-sex couples in all states the right to marry and have marriages recognized, it is being contested in some states. This puts families in a state of legal and social purgatory. These legal decisions affect aging because they affect rights of survivorship, inheritance tax benefits, involvement in medical care, and decision-making about end-of-life care.

Our history as refugees has brought consequences for our convoys. The move to Oregon meant that we had to leave a supportive network in Cleveland, including relationships with Black families and the Black community. Given the distance, we now only see our parents and siblings once a year. Thinking about our parents’ deaths is difficult; talking about it in my family is taboo. My parents’ discomfort with death has made it difficult to discuss their finitude and to anticipate what they may need or want in their final years.

Recently, I asked my parents if they judged their own aging against that of their parents. The thought had never dawned on them. Their choices and opportunities were worlds apart from their parents. And yet, my mother Diann’s aging has been precarious and not so different from her mother’s. Diann was diagnosed with diabetes and extremely high blood pressure at the age of 58, had double by-pass and carotid surgeries at the age of 60, and stays close to home for fear of being too far from medical help. Her illnesses command her attention and shape her daily decisions. Like many women, she might live with chronic illnesses for decades. My father was diagnosed with prostate cancer in his late 60s and had a few years of intense treatment. Otherwise, he is the epitome of active aging.

What life-course principle describes lives woven together through family and other significant relationships?

Accepted answers: linked lives / interdependence / interdependence of lives

Explanation: One person's health, circumstances, and rights can affect another person's aging through relationships.

Evidence: THEMES-INTERDEPENDENCE-001 · Life-course perspective

Read the source passage

The life course perspective sees individual lives are interwoven in webs of interdependence, especially in the family (for illustrations, see Settersten, 2015). In our stories, we placed ourselves in such a fabric, spanning several generations. Interdependence is not only a matter of how individual circumstances are connected, but how societal conditions affect lives through relationships (Elder, 1974). In the family realm, interdependence is also structured through laws and policies (Hagestad & Dykstra, 2016). The complex interactions of interdependent rights and obligations are illustrated in Norwegian policies regarding care leaves. The law provides a given duration of paid leave to care for sick family members. However, when the illness period exceeds the stipulated length of leave, caregivers may risk loss of full pension rights because they will not meet the duration requirements for continuous paid employment. Laws and policies may also block interdependence, as when grandparents are not granted the right to raise grandchildren.

On a personal level, if individuals have supportive and reliable relationships, interdependence can provide stability and continuity in later life, but it can also bring risks, change, and unpredictability. We are not only at the will of our own bodies but also the functioning of others. Relationships can make aging a highly contingent enterprise. It is also important not to limit considerations of interdependence to the here and now. Norms of reciprocity may span long periods: You helped me out 50 years ago, so now it is my turn to help you live with the new precariousness of aging.

There is an extensive literature on gender differences in patterns of interdependence. Moen (2001) argues that women have a “contingency orientation” an attitude of “it depends.” GOH’s mother said, “if we live.” Today, many women, especially in middle generations, say, “if everyone stays healthy.” Because women live longer than men, and have lifetimes of stronger investments in relationships, they also have a more complex web of contingencies.

In our view, interdependence of lives is the best example of how we cannot talk about personal aging without talking about interpersonal ties. A person who is not embedded in a fabric of interdependent lives might face the most naked forms of precariousness.

Which historical transition made lives longer and more predictable, associating finitude increasingly with old age?

Accepted answers: demographic transition / the demographic transition

Explanation: The authors contrast earlier experiences of death across ages with its stronger association with later life in aging societies.

Evidence: THEMES-HISTORY-001 · Historical change

Read the source passage

Our stories show how history leaves individuals with scars, vulnerabilities, and strengths, which in turn affect interpersonal ties. The personal accounts also illustrate how the impact of history transcends family generations. GOH’s views of precariousness and unpredictability reflect her mother’s historical experiences, which in turn, were shaped by the grandparental generation. Indeed, RAS’s views on the same issues have even been influenced by Bertha—through GOH! Consciously or not, we carry and transmit historical influences faced by our parents. It is not just our own cohort that lurs as a force in our lives, but also the cohorts of people who are close to us, especially in the family. This phenomenon is for the most part disregarded in the cohort literature. Our stories also illustrate that, in considering cohorts and periods, attention to herstory alongside history is essential.

Between the starting points of GOH’s parents’ lives and those of RAS’s parents, the demographic transition—sometimes called a revolution—made lives longer and more predictable. In aging societies finitude has, to a dramatic degree, become linked to old age. In this process, the “denial of death” (Becker, 1973) has become stronger.

The historical shift from the risk of death being “ageless” to it being primarily linked to old age becomes personal reality through our mothers. Bertha exemplifies “prerevolutionary” life, focused on precariousness and unpredictability. To her, death was omnipresent, independently of age. Diann is an example of the other side of the watershed in feeling such discomfort with the end of life. Because of GOH’s early years in the war and experience with critical illness, she is more like her mother than her peer Diann in views on life, death, and precariousness. RAS belongs to a cohort from the other side of the demographic divide, but as a middle-aged man sees precariousness quite differently from his mother. This could also be because he has seen close consociates face serious illness, even death, and experienced grave uncertainties due to legal restrictions, prejudice, and stigma. His growing sense of finitude is part of what Neugarten (1968) called the “awareness of middle age.” She observed that between the ages of 40 and 60 years, individuals start thinking in terms of time left rather than time lived. The ability to make this shift itself rests on having a relatively long and predictable life expectancy.

Which transition describes the shift in causes of death from infectious disease toward lifestyle-related illnesses?

Accepted answers: epidemiological transition / the epidemiological transition

Explanation: The essay discusses this shift alongside gendered patterns of mortality, morbidity, and relationships.

Evidence: THEMES-VANGUARDS-001 · Changing disease patterns

Read the source passage

Since Neugarten’s essay, life expectancies have increased and actuarial patterns have become even more predictable. In 1976, Cain put forward a revolutionary proposal that age-based entitlements be given on the basis of a demographic category’s remaining life expectancy. Recently, demographers and policy makers have suggested that time left be used as the basis of certain rights, such as pension (e.g., Vaupel, 2010). If pension policies are changed—a reform that is part of policy discussions in several countries—will this bring altered views of finitude?

Diann also exemplifies a second watershed: the epidemiological transition. In the 20th century, causes of death shifted from infectious disease to lifestyle-related illnesses. With this shift, gender differences in mortality and morbidity widened. Men are more likely to die from acute illness; aging women have more chronic conditions, such as Type 2 diabetes and arthritis. An English saying sums it up: “Women are sicker; men die quicker.” It is also important to keep in mind that, starting in teenage years, women are more used to living “at the will of the body” (Frank, 1991) due to menstrual cycles, pregnancies, births, and menopause. As a result of demographic change, women’s convoys are more characterized by co-longevity and relationships of long duration than is the case for men. This gives old women more available consociates and co-biographers.

Social change does not just “happen”—it is often deliberately created. Both of us have experienced making and living social change, being “the first” or “the only” in choosing new life course options. For GOH, such experiences are closely linked to changes in gender roles. Women, more than men, have used new educational opportunities and have greater attainment in high school and beyond.

RAS has also been part of an educational vanguard within the community where he grew up, but his strongest experiences come from being part of a gay married couple and becoming an adoptive parent. He partnered in an era when this choice was even more difficult than today. A gay couple adopting children, let alone a White couple adopting Black children, was unthinkable a few decades ago. Being vanguards makes building and maintaining support convoys difficult, but essential. Will it also help us cope with aging, given that we have built strategies for resilience in the face of hardship and stigma?

RAS’s vanguard family is linked to a more general phenomenon: men’s increasing engagement in parenting. It is possible that this shift, in which the Nordic countries have been pioneers, may signal a sea change for men in late life. Will new fathers, who created and sustained strong ties to children, have a better old age than earlier cohorts of men? Will they have built stronger convoys?

What term describes people who are among the first to pursue new life-course options without established scripts?

Accepted answers: vanguard / vanguards / a vanguard / social vanguard

Explanation: GOH's educational and occupational pathway and RAS's family formation illustrate being the first or the only.

Evidence: THEMES-VANGUARDS-001 · Social change

Read the source passage

Since Neugarten’s essay, life expectancies have increased and actuarial patterns have become even more predictable. In 1976, Cain put forward a revolutionary proposal that age-based entitlements be given on the basis of a demographic category’s remaining life expectancy. Recently, demographers and policy makers have suggested that time left be used as the basis of certain rights, such as pension (e.g., Vaupel, 2010). If pension policies are changed—a reform that is part of policy discussions in several countries—will this bring altered views of finitude?

Diann also exemplifies a second watershed: the epidemiological transition. In the 20th century, causes of death shifted from infectious disease to lifestyle-related illnesses. With this shift, gender differences in mortality and morbidity widened. Men are more likely to die from acute illness; aging women have more chronic conditions, such as Type 2 diabetes and arthritis. An English saying sums it up: “Women are sicker; men die quicker.” It is also important to keep in mind that, starting in teenage years, women are more used to living “at the will of the body” (Frank, 1991) due to menstrual cycles, pregnancies, births, and menopause. As a result of demographic change, women’s convoys are more characterized by co-longevity and relationships of long duration than is the case for men. This gives old women more available consociates and co-biographers.

Social change does not just “happen”—it is often deliberately created. Both of us have experienced making and living social change, being “the first” or “the only” in choosing new life course options. For GOH, such experiences are closely linked to changes in gender roles. Women, more than men, have used new educational opportunities and have greater attainment in high school and beyond.

RAS has also been part of an educational vanguard within the community where he grew up, but his strongest experiences come from being part of a gay married couple and becoming an adoptive parent. He partnered in an era when this choice was even more difficult than today. A gay couple adopting children, let alone a White couple adopting Black children, was unthinkable a few decades ago. Being vanguards makes building and maintaining support convoys difficult, but essential. Will it also help us cope with aging, given that we have built strategies for resilience in the face of hardship and stigma?

RAS’s vanguard family is linked to a more general phenomenon: men’s increasing engagement in parenting. It is possible that this shift, in which the Nordic countries have been pioneers, may signal a sea change for men in late life. Will new fathers, who created and sustained strong ties to children, have a better old age than earlier cohorts of men? Will they have built stronger convoys?

What concept captures the uncertainty of later life when plans depend on bodies, relationships, and continuing support?

Accepted answers: precariousness / precarity

Explanation: Illness and disability, as well as other people's functioning, can make future plans increasingly conditional.

Evidence: THEMES-PRECARIOUSNESS-001 · Conditions of later life

Read the source passage

Old age is a time when many people face unpredictability and uncertainty. Experiences hinge on many ifs: if we have each other, if we live, if we have our health. Encounters with illness and disability make planning difficult for an increasingly uncertain future. The precariousness of contemporary old age is, in the end, much like the precariousness earlier generations experienced as a natural part of the whole life span. A central aspect of this experience is living with a body that we, to some degree, do not recognize in images presented by physical or social mirrors—they do not match our sense of self.

Some of these changes have less to do with physical functioning than with cultural meanings. Here, there are distinct gender contrasts, as looks have traditionally been at the core of women’s “presentation of self.” Powell Bell (1970) and Sontag (1972) both wrote about the “double standard of aging”: Physical signs of aging make men look distinguished and women look over the hill. Although the “double standard” disadvantages women, aging can also bring a loss of male attractiveness, as culturally defined. Physical frailty may be harder for men, because they place a premium on strength and independence. GOH’s father is an example of an older man who was ready to quit, once his body had clear limitations. The father of RAS’s friend ended his life at 80 years of age, leaving a note saying, “I can’t see. I can’t hear. I can’t drive. I can’t golf. I can’t live.”

As GOH noted in her story, reduced functioning brings the danger of either/or thinking, especially once an individual has been labeled a “patient” or “disabled.” Being a patient becomes a dominant role—a central lens through which we see ourselves and through which others see us. Dichotomous thinking threatens the potential for social participation and contribution—a foundation for a sense of dignity and value. It also deprives society of human resources. It is worth noting that WHO for many years has urged us to not see disability solely as a personal characteristic, but as a relation between individual capacity and environmental demands. In an extensive report on disability (WHO, 2011), the organization explicitly warns against dichotomous thinking.

What research agenda maps how change may shape the relational worlds and options of future aging cohorts?

Accepted answers: anticipatory gerontology

Explanation: It considers how demographic, policy, and cultural developments may create future challenges and possibilities.

Evidence: FINAL-001 · Future research

Read the source passage

Our stories show that we have been strangers, invisible, and faced stigma before becoming old. We have argued that these experiences have in some ways given us a preview into what it is like to be old, a preparation for the precariousness of later life. As aging individuals try to prepare for what lies ahead, honest personal accounts from gerontologists are important. We need to talk about us rather than them, avoiding a dangerous practice of “othering,” which reproduces stigma and agism. By participating in two distinct conversations—one public, one private—gerontologists create a type of “false consciousness” about growing old. Up to now, few have dared to make their private stories public. Gerontological scholars must not be part of a denial process by refusing to be honest about what aging does to the body, or by not acknowledging finitude. In 2012, the EU declared an “International Year of Active Aging and Solidarity between Generations.” Many old people felt that the “active” part of the theme received too much attention and found it discouraging. In their view, it promoted unrealistic expectations. An old Norwegian commented: “It sounds like in order to be active, you bike to a full-time job, and on weekends you climb mountains.”

Our stories revealed an often invisible truth: Personal aging is tangled up in history, and history carries different footprints for men and women. Contrasts in historical circumstances entail dissimilar opportunities for building and maintaining relationships. Our examples also illustrate how our lives are shaped by historical experiences of significant others, such as parents and grandparents. In conducting research and meeting students and patients, gerontologists and geriatricians must be mindful of historical impact on intergenerational webs that shaped the lives of men and women.

Being aware of history not only means looking backwards, it also means looking forward. The future of aging is already here. It can be projected for cohorts who are in middle age and outlined for young adults, youth, and children. Gerontologists must begin to map how the aging of future cohorts presents new challenges and options for society. In other words, we need to engage in what Cain (2003) called anticipatory gerontology. Such efforts include projecting how demographic, policy, and cultural changes will affect the interpersonal worlds of aging men and women, and their options for building and maintaining social ties.

Relationships give aging its contours and content. Biographies are created in exchanges with co-biographers. We use others, and also serve others, as social mirrors. The experience of co-writing this essay has further intensified our awareness of such realities. In the end, our individual and joint stories illustrate that aging is more than personal—it is interpersonal!
