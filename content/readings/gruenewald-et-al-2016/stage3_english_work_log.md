## Evidence review

The assigned editors directly read the local English source supporting their items. The MCQ editor read all 23 segments outside the references, inspected the retained images of Figure 1 and Table 3, and checked all redesigned short answers and their evidence links. The original PDF is absent; this review does not independently establish fidelity to the full publication or resolve the provenance of inconsistent retained source details.

- `ABS-001`, `INTRO-001`, `EC-001`: the present outcomes are older adults' generative desire and self-perceived generative achievement. Structural lag concerns institutions failing to provide roles that match older adults' increased capacity to contribute. EC links school needs with meaningful activity for volunteers. Earlier pilot results and the wider trial's child, cognitive, or health outcomes are not presented as outcomes directly demonstrated by this paper. Program expansion figures are historical to the article.
- `BENEFITS-001`, `ENHANCING-001`, `METHOD-001`: prior observational associations motivate the trial but do not establish a health pathway through generativity. Desire and perceived achievement are distinct, potentially mutually reinforcing constructs. The analysis examines whether these perceptions change, not whether the precise reciprocal mechanism has been demonstrated.
- `PARTICIPANTS-001`, Figure 1: 2,675 people were screened, 702 randomized, 352 assigned to EC, and 350 to control. Sixty-eight EC-assigned participants did not take up placement, leaving 284 who received the intervention. Eligibility included age 60+, literacy, cognitive screening, willingness to volunteer at least 15 hours weekly for at least one school year, and school-placement conditions. Assignment and receipt are not interchangeable. Controls were referred to usual opportunities through CARE, so this was not volunteering versus compulsory inactivity.
- `MATERIALS-001`, `MEASURES-001`: generativity was assessed at baseline and months 4, 12, and 24; other trial contacts occurred at months 8, 16, and 20. Seven desire and six achievement items were scored 1–6 and averaged as separate subscales. Factor analysis supported two correlated factors, and alpha values of .82/.90 concern internal consistency. Neither factor structure nor reliability transforms perceived achievement into an objective measure of contribution.
- `ANALYTIC-ITT-CACE-001`: ITT compares original randomized assignments, retaining noncompliers. CACE models an effect among compliers using observed EC compliance and latent potential compliance in controls. These are different estimands. The latter is not an ordinary comparison of actual high-hour volunteers with everyone else and requires additional assumptions.
- `ANALYTIC-ASSUMPTIONS-001`: the materials identify CACE's dependence on assumptions and preserve the warnings attached to individual models. The SUTVA question concerns interference between participants, without claiming that randomization alone proves its absence. The paper's account of program access conflicts with its flowchart, as detailed below.
- `ANALYTIC-EXPOSURE-001`: the analysis text defines graded compliance using 20th/40th/60th/80th percentiles of observed cumulative service hours at each assessment. Hours were not randomized, and changing thresholds changes which potential compliers are targeted. The 15-hour target is not an estimated optimum. Missing outcomes were modeled directly for CACE and multiply imputed for ITT; small baseline covariate gaps received separate single regression-based imputation. Follow-up completion rates are distinct from the smaller counts with usable scale responses.
- `RESULTS-DESCRIPTIVE-001`, `RESULTS-MODELS-001`, Table 3: both ITT outcomes favored EC significantly at all three assessments. Higher-threshold CACE models generally showed larger effect sizes, but the pattern is not strictly monotonic and not every CACE estimate is significant. The source contains assumption-count and table-label discrepancies, so questions do not require accepting disputed cells as definitive facts.
- `DISCUSSION-001`, `LIMITATIONS-001`, `STRENGTHS-001`, `CONCLUSION-001`: high baseline scores can limit room for improvement. Attrition, a predominantly African American sample that was 85% female, and extensive other volunteering affect interpretation. Recruitment of older minority adults is also a strength. Whether intergenerational service is uniquely beneficial relative to other high-intensity volunteering remains open, as do health mediation and optimal service dose.

The short-answer owner reduced numerical recall to three questions: 702 randomized participants, the target of 15 hours weekly, and follow-ups at 4/12/24 months. New concept questions address noncompliance, factor analysis, internal consistency, SUTVA, and the qualified dose-response interpretation. All revised evidence links were directly checked. Disputed assumption counts and percentile labels are not required as short answers.

## Source reporting issues

1. `RESULTS-MODELS-001` lists four models with assumption concerns, then refers to these three models. The four listed combinations are desire at month 24/20th percentile and achievement at month 4/60th, month 12/40th, and month 24/40th. The precise count is not tested as an uncontested fact. Learning materials retain that some CACE models require caution.
2. The analysis text and Table 3 headings use the 60th percentile, but the table footnote calls the third threshold the 65th percentile at 12 and 24 months. Direct image inspection confirms the difference. MCQ question 11 expressly asks about the analysis text and notes the conflicting footnote; OX and short answers emphasize observed thresholds without requiring the disputed label.
3. `ANALYTIC-ASSUMPTIONS-001` says controls could not access EC, supporting its no-always-taker/no-defier account. Figure 1 explicitly reports 11 control-to-intervention crossovers. The retained image confirms this is not only an accessibility-description error. The materials distinguish the intended referral condition from actual flow and do not claim crossover was absent. The local source does not explain how those crossovers reconcile with the model-access argument.
4. Table 3's achievement effect sizes at four months are .080, .059, .969, and 1.069 across increasing thresholds. The first step decreases. The materials therefore say that higher-threshold effects were generally larger, not that every adjacent threshold increased or that all CACE results were significant.
5. Table 3 prints the 12-month/40th-percentile achievement estimate as .009 with a .059 standard error and a double-star significance mark. That mark does not align with the apparent estimate-to-standard-error ratio. The retained image confirms the cell; its coefficient, standard error, and significance annotation are not repaired or used as a correct numerical answer. The paper's broader ITT result can be discussed separately from that unresolved cell.

The source text, translations, and figures were preserved. These issues constrain interpretation of specific source claims; this learning-material review does not independently reanalyze the trial or reconstruct missing documentation.
