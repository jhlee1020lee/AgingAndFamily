## Evidence review

Each assigned editor directly read the local English source supporting their items. The MCQ editor read all 17 non-reference segments, examined the retained Table 2 and Table 3 images, and checked all final short-answer evidence links and the revised OX 12 link. Original PDFs are absent; this review does not independently establish fidelity to the entire original publication or verify every primary study summarized in it.

- `ABS-001`, `INTRO-001`: the review compares explicit definitions of a good death from patient, family, and healthcare-provider perspectives. This differs from assessing the agreement between dying preferences and observations of how death occurred. The objective is neither a comparison of survival across care settings nor a causal test of communication or policy interventions.
- `METHOD-DATA-001`, `METHOD-SELECTION-001`: searches covered PubMed and PsycINFO through November 2015, with reference-list checking. Eligible articles were English-language, peer-reviewed empirical studies defining or measuring good death as a main aim or outcome. Related topics alone did not meet that criterion. The final 36 studies included 27 qualitative, five quantitative, and four mixed-methods articles.
- `METHOD-SELECTION-001`: three of the nine quantitative or mixed-methods studies used standardized good-death tools, while six developed their own measures. The presence of named instruments does not imply a common measure across studies. MCQ 4 now tests measurement heterogeneity instead of recalling three instrument names.
- `METHOD-CODING-001`: two authors independently read and coded articles, refined 38 initial themes to 11 core themes through consensus, and used Other for items not fitting specific themes. Coding was informed by grounded theory. Independent agreement and later resolution of disagreements are distinct steps. The reported kappa .896 is retained as a reliability value; it does not validate one universal concept of a good death.
- `METHOD-CODING-001`: 20 patient, 10 family, and 18 provider article-group counts overlap. Twenty-nine articles contributed to one group, two to two groups, and five to all three: 36 unique articles and 48 group assignments. These are not 48 independent studies. Patient-perspective samples also included general-population participants; family accounts included pre- and postbereavement perspectives; provider categories combined multiple professions.
- `METHOD-ANALYSES-001`, `RESULTS-STAKEHOLDERS-001`: the review did not conduct a formal meta-analysis or apply study weights. Table 3 frequencies have articles as their units and stakeholder-category article totals as denominators. For example, quality of life appears in seven of 20 patient-perspective articles, yielding 35%. This is not a pooled individual response rate, a causal effect, a statistically established priority ranking, or evidence that a theme is unimportant to any particular patient.
- `RESULTS-SAMPLE-001`: age reporting mixed means and ranges and was incomplete. The 50% statement concerns patient-perspective articles including people over 60, not the percentage of individual respondents over 60. The source's mean age of 89.7 is questionable relative to its displayed study ages and has not been independently resolved. It is not used as a verified answer or silently corrected.
- `RESULTS-THEMES-001`: Table 2 distinguishes treatment preferences from life completion, emotional well-being, family, dignity, and other themes. Treatment preferences include potentially different wishes across people, such as limiting life prolongation, believing available treatments were used, and retaining treatment control. The review does not prescribe every listed preference to each patient.
- `RESULTS-STAKEHOLDERS-001`: dying-process preferences and pain-free status show strong convergence across article groups. MCQ 11 tests the shared themes rather than complex numerical option sets. Its explanation preserves the consistent within-group values: 100/100/94% for dying-process preferences and 85/90/83% for pain-free status. These still refer to articles, not people.
- `DISCUSSION-CONSENSUS-001`, `DISCUSSION-DIGNITY-001`: differences in quality-of-life and dignity reporting may involve differing definitions, retrospective family accounts, overlap across themes, and difficulty articulating values. These are possible explanations. The review does not establish family proxy accuracy, an inherent perspective effect, or lesser concern for dignity among patients.
- `RESULTS-STAKEHOLDERS-001`, `DISCUSSION-DIGNITY-001`: patient-versus-family spirituality values are article-level frequencies, 13/20 and 5/10. OX 12 was linked to `RESULTS-STAKEHOLDERS-001` because that segment gives the explicit denominators. A reported difference does not establish a statistically significant difference in individual preferences. Historical service-context discussion is not generalized to current provision in every setting.
- `DISCUSSION-LIMITATIONS-001`, `DISCUSSION-PSYCHOLOGICAL-001`: inconsistent measures and respondent descriptions, missing interview-to-death timing, underrepresented prebereaved families, combined provider professions, and sparse experience/training data limit inference. English-language and publication restrictions further narrow coverage. The authors call for greater attention to psychological, social, and spiritual concerns alongside physical symptoms; this is not evidence that physical comfort is unimportant.
- `FUTURE-001`: proposed dialogue and future research prioritize the patient's own values within discussions with families and providers. The authors seek diverse, well-characterized samples, validated measures, and clearer sampling. The findings do not establish that a universal checklist or a particular communication program improves outcomes.

## Retained-source uncertainties

- `METHOD-CODING-001` prints the malformed p-value `p < 0.0.000` next to kappa .896. The p-value is not reused as a valid statistic or repaired by inference. The short answer retains only the reported kappa coefficient.
- `RESULTS-SAMPLE-001` reports mean age 89.7 despite heterogeneous and incomplete study-age descriptions. The learning materials explain the reporting limitation without treating that mean as a settled summary.
- The retained Table 3 and its transcription print provider spirituality as 9 (59) with a denominator of 18. Nine of 18 is 50%, so the printed count and percentage conflict. This cell is excluded from answers and quantitative comparisons; the original is preserved.
- `ABS-001` and `RESULTS-THEMES-001` report pain-free status in 81% of articles, while `DISCUSSION-CONSENSUS-001` says both pain-free status and dying-process preferences appeared in more than 85%. That broader statement is inconsistent with the earlier figure. Where necessary, the materials identify the results-section scope and disclose the unresolved difference. The general convergence of themes is retained without making the disputed threshold an answer.

The short-answer owner replaced most numerical and name recall with eligibility, coding tradition, dignity, weighting, overlap, heterogeneity, core themes, missing timing, and underrepresented family perspectives. The sole numeric answer is the reported kappa .896. All reassigned evidence links were checked against the final questions and explanations.
